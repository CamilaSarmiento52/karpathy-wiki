# Bitter lesson
Richard Sutton's essay "The Bitter Lesson", which he says has become a bit of biblical text in frontier LLM circles, where researchers ask whether an idea is "bitter lesson pilled", meaning arranged to benefit from added computation for free, as a proxy for whether it will work ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). His own writing on it is a response to Sutton's appearance on the Dwarkesh podcast ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

## What he says
**What the researchers assume.** He reports that frontier researchers assume LLMs are highly bitter-lesson pilled, because LLM scaling laws go up and to the right with compute on the x-axis ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). That is the researchers' assumption as he describes it, not a claim of his own ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

**Sutton's position, as he reports it.** Sutton, he writes, is not sure LLMs are bitter-lesson pilled at all, since they train on human-generated and finite data, and asks what to do when it runs out and how to keep out human bias ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Sutton calls himself a "classicist" and evokes Turing's "child machine", which learns through experience by interacting with the world, with no pretraining on web pages and no supervised finetuning, which Sutton points out is absent in animals ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Even pretraining treated as a prior before RL finetuning is, in Sutton's view, tainted with human bias and off course; he likens Sutton's view to AlphaZero, which never saw human games, beating AlphaGo, which was initialized from them ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). In Sutton's world view, as he puts it, there is only RL interaction with a world, with rewards partly environment-specific and partly intrinsic (fun, curiosity, prediction quality in a world model), and the agent always learns at test time ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Sutton is more interested in what humans share with animals than in what sets them apart, and the line he quotes to close that summary, about understanding a squirrel being nearly enough, is Sutton's, not his ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). He thinks Dwarkesh and Sutton partly speak past each other, because Sutton has a very different architecture in mind, one whose principles LLMs break ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

**Where he agrees.** He calls Sutton a great guest and likes that the field keeps entropy of thought, since AI has gone through too many discrete transitions of dominant approach to lose that ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). He agrees that frontier LLMs are full of human input and not platonically bitter-lesson pilled; that is a separate claim ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

**Animal brains are not a blank slate.** Two "example proofs" are usually offered that a learn-from-experience algorithm exists, AlphaZero and animals, and he doubts both transfer ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Go is so simple and closed an environment that the analogous formulation in messy reality is hard to see; he loves Go but says it is "essentially a harder version of tic tac toe" ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Animals arise by a very different computational process, under constraints the industry does not have, and "Animal brains are nowhere near the blank slate they appear to be at birth." ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Much of what is called learning is in his view maturation, and what clearly is learning is more like finetuning on something powerful and preexisting ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). His example is a baby zebra that runs and follows its mother within a few dozen minutes of birth, which he says cannot be learned tabula rasa: the brain has a powerful initialization encoded in DNA, trained by evolution's "outer loop", and a zebra spasming at random, as an RL policy at initialization would, would not get far ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). What fills that role for LLMs is [[pretraining-is-our-crappy-evolution]] ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

**Animals do not do supervised learning.** He says Sutton is right "in the strong sense" that supervised finetuning is absent in animals: animals observe demonstrations, but their actions are not forced or teleoperated by other animals ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). In the Appendix he writes "I agree with Sutton that animals don't do supervised learning." ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §Appendix). The closest case he can think of is holding a child's hand to show a spoon motion, and even that may be observation rather than training; such cases are very rare, while for LLMs supervised learning is the default mode in pretraining and SFT ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §Appendix). The human analogue, he suggests, is RL with an example in context: given a math problem and a human example solution, solve it and get a reward of 1 if correct, "It's not SFT, it's RL." ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §Appendix).

**Still worth being inspired by animals.** He thinks LLM agents are algorithmically missing several powerful ideas that could be adapted from animal intelligence, and that the bitter lesson is correct as something platonic to pursue, saying both with double-digit percent uncertainty ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). He calls the pod solid "real talk" for frontier researchers who may be too much in exploit mode, and says there is a very good chance of more powerful paradigms than exhaustive benchbuilding and benchmaxxing ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). The ideas he names are on [[llm-self-improvement]] ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

**In the Dwarkesh interview (published 2025-10-17).** Asked why AI should not learn from scratch like an animal, he refers to his write-up of the Sutton podcast and says he is very careful with animal analogies, since animals came from a very different optimisation process; the zebra running minutes after birth is baked in, not reinforcement learning ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @08:14, @08:49). Sutton's framework, he says, is to build animals; a single algorithm you could run on the internet that learns everything would be wonderful, but he is not sure it exists, and animals do not do that, because they have the outer loop of evolution ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @09:25, @10:04).
