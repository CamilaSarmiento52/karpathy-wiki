# Scaling laws
In his framing, the accuracy of an LLM's next-word prediction is a remarkably smooth, predictable function of just two variables, N, the number of parameters, and D, the amount of text trained on ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @25:28, @26:02).

## What he says
Given only those two numbers, he says, you can predict with remarkable confidence the accuracy you will reach, and the trends show no sign of topping out, so a bigger model trained on more text will improve next-word prediction with high confidence ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @26:02). Next-word accuracy is not what you actually care about, but empirically it correlates with the evaluations you do care about; going from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4, all the tests he shows improve ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @26:35, @27:10). This, he says, is what fundamentally drives the gold rush for bigger GPU clusters and more data; algorithmic progress is a nice bonus that organizations still invest in heavily ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @27:10).

**Note:** in State of GPT (published 2023-05-25) he made a related point about data: LLaMA 65B is more powerful than GPT-3 175B because it trained on 1.4 trillion tokens rather than 300 billion, so parameter count alone is no guide to a model's power ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @03:56; [[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @25:28).

**Scale first, but not enough (Sequoia, 2024-03-26).** Asked by the host whether scale is all that matters, he says scale is definitely number one, the first principal component, though there are details to get right; dataset preparation and algorithms are compute-efficiency gains that matter a lot ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @09:38, @10:10). Scale sets something like a speed limit: without it you cannot train the massive models, though finetuning may need less ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @10:10). Even with money and scale, training is a very complicated distributed-optimisation problem on tens of thousands of GPUs failing at random, the infrastructure is creaking and the talent is scarce ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @10:43, @11:15). It is not obvious to him that someone given a ton of money or GPUs could produce one of these models; it takes expertise in infrastructure, algorithms and data ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @11:15).

**A property of the Transformer (No Priors, 2024-09-05).** He says scaling laws are to a large extent a property of the Transformer: stacked LSTMs did not give clean scaling laws, and the Transformer was the first thing that just scales ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @14:49). On the data wall and the cost of another generation of scale, he says the architecture no longer holds us back fundamentally; the dataset and the loss function have become the bottlenecks ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @15:56, @16:29). On model size he expects a small cognitive core may suffice ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @27:27).

**The GPU gold rush (Deep Dive, 2025-02-05, background).** He says big tech's demand for GPUs to train language models is what drove Nvidia to $3.4 trillion (as of 2025-02-05), and calls getting enough GPUs the gold rush; more GPUs let you process the data and iterate faster and train a bigger network ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @41:23, @41:56). **Note:** he states no scaling law there, so the lecture is background on this page ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @41:56).

**Note:** in a post dated 2025-10-01 he reports that frontier researchers assume LLMs are highly bitter-lesson pilled because scaling laws go up and to the right with compute, and Richard Sutton's counterpoint that the data is human and finite; he expects more powerful paradigms but does not say scaling stops working, so the post is background here; see [[bitter-lesson]] ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

**Training past Chinchilla (nanochat d34, 2025-11-20).** His d34 run is "2X longtrained" compared to Chinchilla: the tokens-to-parameters ratio (he writes "param:token") is overridden from 20 (comparison, Chinchilla) to 40 (setting), so the model is trained longer than compute optimal, "just to squeeze a bit more capability into a bit smaller package" ([[github-2025-11-20-nanochat-d34-model]] §intro). The script derives 88,683,315,200 training tokens and 169,150 iterations (estimates, calculated by the script) from 524,288 tokens per batch and sequence length 2048 (settings) ([[github-2025-11-20-nanochat-d34-model]] §intro). Here "compute optimal" means Chinchilla's ratio of 20 ([[github-2025-11-20-nanochat-d34-model]] §intro).

**A family on one dial (miniseries v1, 2026-01-07).** The correct way to think about LLMs, he says, is that you are optimising not one model but a family controlled by a single dial, the compute you wish to spend, for monotonically better results; that allows careful scaling-law science and the confidence that, when you pay for "the big run", the extrapolation will work ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §intro (Why miniseries)). If code, architecture, optimisation and scaling laws are right, the curves of such a series should not intersect ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §intro (Miniseries v1)). At nanochat's scale training is in the infinite-data regime, with no overfitting and validation loss falling indefinitely as you train longer, so "how long to train a d12" is the wrong question; the real one is whether, for a FLOP budget, a small model trained long or a big model trained short does better ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Scaling laws)). His procedure, fixed-FLOP sweeps over depth fitted to U-shaped curves, is [[fit-your-own-scaling-law]] ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Scaling laws)).

**What his fits show (2026-01-07).** Like Chinchilla, optimal parameters and tokens each scale as compute to about 0.5, so the optimal tokens-to-parameters ratio is constant; see [[nanochat-reproduces-chinchillas-equal-scaling-of-parameters-and-tokens]] ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Scaling laws)). Chinchilla measures that constant at 20 (comparison); nanochat's fits give about 8 (measured, fit), which he says might come from nanochat specifics such as Muon or from the small model sizes ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Scaling laws)). He reports that Kaplan et al. found a size-dependent ratio because of a major learning-rate-decay bug that Chinchilla pointed out, and his code comment says his nanochat experiments confirm that counting all parameters, Chinchilla's way, gives the much cleaner law than excluding embeddings, as Kaplan did ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Scaling laws)). A fit over d12 and up gives `CORE = 1.0000 - 3.7555 * FLOPs^(-0.0344)` (estimate, fit), which he uses to extrapolate to GPT-2 and GPT-3; he warns against reading much into it, since it spans orders of magnitude from few points and assumes d20's utilisation at scale, though as a sanity check it predicts 5.7e23 FLOPs for GPT-3 against about 3e23 for the real run (estimate; comparison) ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Miniseries v1 CORE scores)); the post's text calls it a parameters-to-CORE fit while the formula uses FLOPs, and the wiki gives the formula as printed ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Miniseries v1 CORE scores)). That extrapolation table is headed "12:1 D:N ratio" while the text gives 8; the post does not reconcile them ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Miniseries v1 CORE scores)). **Note:** the 2025-11-20 d34 post calls Chinchilla's 20 "compute optimal" ([[github-2025-11-20-nanochat-d34-model]] §intro); this post measures about 8 for nanochat at its code state of 2026-01-07 ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Scaling laws)). Each is given with its date.

**About 10 (2026-01-31).** In the record run's training script the tokens-to-parameters ratio is 10.5 for compute optimal, or 12 for the speedrun, a slight overtrain (settings), needing about 8.8B FineWeb-edu tokens in total ([[github-2026-01-31-nanochat-beating-gpt-2]] §Training Script). Among what worked he lists that they empirically measured the optimal ratio at about 10 (measured), adding "It's important to do the actual experiment on your own network." ([[github-2026-01-31-nanochat-beating-gpt-2]] §What Worked). **Note:** the 2026-01-07 fits gave about 8 and the 2025-11-20 d34 post used Chinchilla's 20 as compute optimal; each figure is given with its date and code state, not as a revision ([[github-2026-01-07-nanochat-miniseries-v1]] §Details (Scaling laws); [[github-2025-11-20-nanochat-d34-model]] §intro; [[github-2026-01-31-nanochat-beating-gpt-2]] §What Worked).
