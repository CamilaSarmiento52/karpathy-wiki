# Transformer architecture
In his framing, all that happens in GPT is that a sequence of indices feeds into a Transformer and a probability distribution over the next index in the sequence comes out; the model is not complicated, and most of the implementation complexity is being clever with batching, across examples and over sequence length, for efficiency ([[github-2023-01-08-mingpt-readme]] §intro).

## What he says
The minGPT README closes with his condensed notes on the OpenAI papers; the numbers below are his reading notes on those papers, not statements about his own models ([[github-2023-01-08-mingpt-readme]] §References). On GPT-1 he records a 12-layer decoder-only Transformer with masked self-attention, 768-dimensional states, 12 heads, a 3,072-dimensional feed-forward layer, learned position embeddings, GELU, BPE with 40,000 merges, dropout 0.1 and about 117M parameters ([[github-2023-01-08-mingpt-readme]] §References (GPT-1)). On GPT-2 he notes LayerNorm moved to the input of each sub-block plus one after the final block, residual weights scaled by 1/√N at initialisation, a vocabulary of 50,257, a context of 1,024, batch 512, and 48 layers with d_model 1,600 for about 1.542B parameters; he flags that he could not find the 1/√N residual initialisation in OpenAI's released GPT-2 code, only partly in image-gpt ([[github-2023-01-08-mingpt-readme]] §References (GPT-2)). On GPT-3 he notes the same architecture as GPT-2 with alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention, a feed-forward width of four times d_model, a context of 2,048, Adam β2 = 0.95, weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0, linear warmup then cosine decay to 10%, a batch size ramped up during training, and a largest model of 96 layers, 96 heads, d_model 12,288 and 175B parameters ([[github-2023-01-08-mingpt-readme]] §References (GPT-3)). On Image GPT he notes pixels in raster order with a 512-colour k-means palette, GPT-2 model code with Sparse-Transformer-style layer-dependent initialisation and zero-initialised logit projections, no dropout and no weight decay, with open questions of his own left in the notes ([[github-2023-01-08-mingpt-readme]] §References (Image GPT)).

**The block.** In the GPT lecture, the paper's feed-forward part is a simple per-position MLP; he adds it because after attention the tokens looked at each other but had no time to think on what they found ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:24:48, @1:25:20). Self-attention is the communication; the feed-forward, applied to each token independently, is where they think on the gathered data ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:25:52). A block intersperses communication (multi-head self-attention) with computation (feed-forward); the Transformer groups and replicates these blocks ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:26:26, @1:26:59). Stacking blocks sequentially without further ideas did not train well, which he attributes to deep-network optimisation issues, so he borrows residual connections and LayerNorm from the paper rather than tuning ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:27:31, @1:28:02). The feed-forward inner layer is four times the embedding dimension, following the paper's 512 to 2,048 ratio ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:31:23, @1:31:57).

**Residual connections.** Skip or residual connections come from the 2015 residual-learning paper and add transformed data back onto the previous features; he visualises a residual pathway from which computation forks off and projects back by addition ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:28:36, @1:29:11). Because addition distributes gradients equally to both branches, gradients hop through every addition node from the loss to the input, a gradient superhighway ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:29:11, @1:29:46). Residual blocks are initialised to contribute very little, so at init the pathway is almost unobstructed and blocks come online during optimisation ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:29:46, @1:30:19). Implemented as x = x + sa(x) and x = x + ffwd(x), with a linear projection back into the residual pathway after both ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:30:19, @1:30:51, @1:31:23). With residuals and the 4x inner layer, validation loss reaches about 2.08 and train loss starts to get ahead of validation, a little overfitting ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:31:57, @1:32:30).

**LayerNorm.** LayerNorm is very similar to batch norm, which made each neuron zero-mean unit-variance across the batch; changing the normalisation dimension from columns to rows turns his makemore BatchNorm1d into LayerNorm, normalising each example's vector ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:32:30, @1:33:04, @1:33:37, @1:34:10). Because the computation no longer spans examples, the running buffers and the train/test distinction go away; gamma and beta remain ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:34:43). Unlike the original paper's add-and-norm after the transformation, it is now common to apply LayerNorm before, the pre-norm formulation, which he implements as a slight deviation ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:35:19). Mean and variance are taken over the embedding dimension per token; batch and time act as batch dimensions ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:36:26). LayerNorm improves validation loss from about 2.08 to 2.06; he expects more help in bigger, deeper networks, and a final LayerNorm goes before the output linear layer ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:36:59).

**Decoder-only.** The result is a decoder-only Transformer with the major pieces of the original paper in place: no encoder and no cross-attention block, just self-attention and feed-forward ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:37:35, @1:42:35). It is decoder-only because text generation here is unconditioned, just imitating a dataset ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:43:07, @1:45:55). The original paper used encoder-decoder because it was a machine-translation paper, reading French tokens and decoding English between start and end tokens; the encoder runs a Transformer with no triangular mask so all tokens talk fully, and the decoder adds cross-attention where queries come from x but keys and values come from the encoder output ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:43:39, @1:44:12, @1:44:48, @1:45:22). This, he says, is exactly as done in GPT ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:45:55). The scaled lecture model has six layers, 384 embedding dimensions, six heads, block size 256, batch size 64 and dropout 0.2 ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:39:13, @1:39:46). GPT-3's architecture, he says, is nearly identical to what the lecture implemented, only far larger ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:50:56).

**History (CS25, 2023).** In his Stanford CS25 lecture he likes the 2003 neural language model as the first popular application of neural nets to language modelling: an MLP that takes three words and predicts a distribution over the fourth ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @15:00, @15:32). The 2014 sequence-to-sequence paper handled variable-length input and output for translation with an encoder LSTM whose context vector conditions a decoder LSTM ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @15:32, @16:05). People quickly identified the encoder bottleneck: packing the whole source sentence into one vector seemed like too much information ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @16:39). The 2014 paper on jointly learning to align and translate let the decoder soft-search back over the encoder's words, with a context vector that is a softmax-weighted sum of encoder hidden states; he calls these the current modern equations of attention and, as far as he knows, the first use of the word for the mechanism ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @16:39, @17:11, @17:44, @18:17). He relays an email from that paper's first author: the idea came from wanting the decoder to learn where to put a cursor on the source, it worked on the first try, and the name "attention" came from Yoshua Bengio on a final pass, replacing "RNN search" ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @18:17, @18:50, @19:23, @19:57). That account is the author's, as he relays it.

He reads the 2017 paper as deleting everything except attention and combining several things at once, positional encodings because attention operates over sets, the ResNet residual structure, attention interspersed with MLPs, layer norms, multiple heads and a 4x MLP expansion, into a very good local minimum in architecture space; he calls it a landmark paper ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @19:57, @20:30, @21:04, @21:39). It read as a random machine-translation paper, and he says authors he talked to were unaware of the impact at the time ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @49:02, @49:34). Asked about precursors, he likens the Transformer to a ResNet with a self-attention block added alongside the existing MLP block ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @48:28).

**Resilience.** He says papers have tried changing all kinds of details and nothing sticks because the design is quite good; the change he knows of that stuck is moving the layer norms to the pre-norm position, so today's GPTs are basically the 2017 architecture ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @21:39). Positional encodings have also changed, rotary and relative encodings being more common now, but for the most part it has proven very resilient ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @22:11). Five years on, he says, it has not changed even though everyone is trying to change it ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @49:02). **Note:** this matches the GPT lecture's remark that GPT-3 is nearly identical to the lecture model, only larger ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @21:39; [[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:50:56).

**Resilient, but not final (Sequoia, 2024-03-26).** He calls the Transformer amazing and says he would not have seen it coming: before it he expected an insane diversification of neural networks, and instead it is all the same model ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @33:51). He says the Transformer was designed for the GPU, an extremely parallelisable architecture that broke the RNN's sequential dependencies through attention, with predecessors such as the Neural GPU ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @34:57). It has proven remarkably resilient, the original and today's being not super different, but he thinks changes are very likely ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @35:30).

**Encoder, decoder, encoder-decoder.** Encoder tokens are fully connected; decoder tokens connect to all encoder states and only to earlier decoder tokens, a triangular structure, and cross-attention consumes features from the top of the encoder ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @26:06, @26:40). In nanoGPT's code, deleting the mask line makes an encoder, and adding a cross-attention line whose queries come from x and keys and values from the top of the encoder makes an encoder-decoder ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @43:13, @43:47, @44:20). GPT is decoder-only, BERT encoder-only and T5 encoder-decoder; BERT trains with masking and other denoising objectives rather than next-token prediction ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @44:20, @44:52). In nanoGPT's block the residual pathway feeds a layer norm and self-attention (communicate), then the MLP (compute), a two-layer net with a GELU nonlinearity applied per node ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @37:08, @37:41, @38:17). Context length is finite, typically 1,024 or 2,048 tokens in such models, and extending it is hard because attention is quadratic ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @42:39).

**Flexible inputs.** He calls the way Transformers were brought to vision "kind of ridiculous" from a computer-vision perspective, chopping an image into squares and feeding them in so the model must rediscover structure, yet the simplest baseline works fairly well ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @52:43, @53:17). At Tesla, adding radar, map, vehicle-type or audio inputs to a convnet raised hard questions of where to feed them; with a Transformer you chop everything up, add it to the set and let self-attention work out the communication ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @54:23, @54:57). This frees neural nets from laying out computation in Euclidean space, which he finds beautiful ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @54:57, @55:30).

**Why it works.** He argues it is expressive in the forward pass, optimizable thanks to residual connections and layer norms, and efficient as a shallow, wide graph suited to GPU parallelism ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @58:12, @58:44). RNNs can in principle implement arbitrary programs but are long, thin, serial compute graphs that are poorly optimizable and inefficient, whereas a Transformer has few hops from supervision to inputs and processes every word in parallel ([[talk-2023-05-19-stanford-cs25-transformers]] @1:02:29, @1:03:01, @1:03:35).

**GPT-2 as the template (llm.c, 2024-05-28).** He says GPT-3 changed little about the model relative to GPT-2, he thinks only the context size from 1024 to 2048, and that many later LLMs strongly resemble GPT-2: Llama 3, architecturally, differs by a change of MLP non-linearity and the addition of RoPE relative positional encoding ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (FAQ)). The llama2.c README lists the notable changes from GPT-1/2 in Llama as RoPE relative positional embeddings instead of absolute learned ones, the "a bit more fancy" SwiGLU non-linearity in the MLP, RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm, `bias=False` on all Linear layers, and optional multiquery attention; its 110M model is GPT-1 sized and, as he puts it, equivalent to GPT-2 small except for a max context of 1024 instead of 2048 ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §models).

**GPT-2 rebuilt in PyTorch (GPT-2 lecture, 2024-06-09).** The GPT-2 miniseries runs from 124M to 1,558M parameters; he says the parameter table in the GPT-2 paper is wrong and points to OpenAI's repo noting the error ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @01:05). The 124M model has 12 layers and 768 channels, properties of the released model ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @01:38). GPT-2 is decoder-only: the original Transformer's encoder, and the cross-attention that used it, are gone ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @13:55). Per the GPT-2 paper the layer norms move before the attention and MLP and an extra layer norm sits before the final classifier, which projects 768 channels to the 50,257-token vocabulary with no bias ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @14:29, @16:48). He wants one clean residual stream from the supervision down to the tokens, because addition passes gradients to both branches unchanged, and calls normalisations inside the residual path undesirable ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @18:28, @19:04).

He describes attention as where the 1,024 tokens communicate, a pooling or weighted-sum reduce, and the MLP as acting on each token individually, a map, so the Transformer is a repeated map-reduce in which each block refines the residual stream ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @19:38, @20:12). He implements all heads in one attention module, treating the head count as a batch dimension; it is mathematically equivalent to the concatenated heads of the earlier lecture, only more efficient in PyTorch ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @24:38, @25:46, @26:20, @26:53). His complete GPT-2 is under 100 lines, against roughly 2,000 in Hugging Face's `modeling_gpt2.py` ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @27:27).

In his summary of the papers, GPT-2 and GPT-3 are very similar architectures: the context length goes from 1,024 to 2,048, and GPT-3 was trained longer on more data, up to 175B parameters against GPT-2's 1.6B ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:15:55, @2:16:28). To be faithful to GPT-3 one would set sequence length 2,048 and micro-batch 32 so a step stays at about half a million tokens ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:52:03).

In the 1.6B post he again says GPT-3 of the same size is pretty much GPT-2's architecture with minor differences (context length 1024 to 2048) but trained for 300B tokens, and that GPT-2's release had weights but very few details while GPT-3's had details but no weights ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Evals), §intro (Args guide)). The 1558M model is `-e "d48"`, a depth-48 GPT-2 initialised from scratch, with 1,557,686,400 parameters per the log ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Args guide), §intro (Training)).

**A differentiable computer (No Priors, 2024-09-05).** He says the Transformer is not just another neural net but an amazing, extremely general one, which he thinks of as a general-purpose training computer, a differentiable computer: given billions of inputs and outputs and trained with backpropagation, it arranges itself into a thing that does the task ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @14:49, @15:24). Four or five existing innovations were put together into it, which he says Google did in its paper: residual connections, layer normalisation, the attention block, and the absence of saturating nonlinearities like tanh, which kill gradient signals ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @15:24, @15:56). Since the Llama release the major change is RoPE positional encodings; everything else is about plus 3%, over the last five years or so ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @16:29, @17:01).

**Better than the brain in some ways.** Cautioning against brain analogies, he still thinks Transformers are better than the human brain in a bunch of ways, a more efficient system that works less well mostly because of data, as a first approximation ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @20:13, @20:46). His examples: a Transformer memorises a sequence after a single forward-backward pass, which a human cannot; there is a good chance gradient-based optimisation is more efficient than the brain in some ways; and human working memory is very small next to a Transformer's ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @20:46, @21:18). It is not obvious the brain does backpropagation, and it works under many constraints; what we have is potentially better than the brain, just not there yet ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @21:51). **Note:** this is learning and memory efficiency; the energy comparison on [[ai-is-far-less-energy-efficient-than-the-brain]] is a different measure, and he does not compare the two ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @13:27; [[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @20:46).

**A fixed mathematical expression (Deep Dive, 2025-02-05).** For a general audience he describes the network as inputs and parameters mixed in a mathematical expression of simple operations; architecture research designs expressions that are expressive, optimizable and parallelizable, and they are not complex ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @21:52, @22:27). Using a web visualization of a Transformer of about 85,000 parameters he walks through embeddings, attention, MLP, layer norms and the output softmax ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @22:59, @23:33, @24:06). He warns against thinking of these values as biological neurons: the expression is stateless, with no memory, while biological neurons are complex dynamical processes ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @24:40, @25:12). A modern network has on the order of 100 layers, so the compute per token is finite and roughly fixed ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:49:28).
