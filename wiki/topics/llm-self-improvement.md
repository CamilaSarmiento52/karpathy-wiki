# LLM self-improvement
The open question, in his framing, of what an AlphaGo-style second stage would be for language models: going beyond imitating humans by improving against an automatic reward ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @38:14, @39:52).

## What he says
AlphaGo, he recalls, had two stages: imitating expert human games, which can only reach the best human, and then self-improvement by playing millions of games against a simple automatic reward, winning, which took it past the top humans in 40 days ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @38:14, @38:46, @39:20). LLMs today, he says, only do stage one, imitating human labelers, so it would be hard to go above human response accuracy ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @39:20, @39:52). The main challenge for a step two in open language modelling is the lack of a fast, simple reward criterion in the general case ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @39:52). In narrow domains such a reward function could be achievable, so he thinks self-improvement there may be possible; the general case remains open ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @40:27).

**Step one of AlphaGo (Sequoia, 2024-03-26).** Asked whether next-word predictors can become a physicist, he says current models are not good enough and there are big rocks in capability still to turn: the field has done AlphaGo's imitation-learning step and not its RL step, which is what made AlphaGo work ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @24:59, @25:32). He calls data collection for ChatGPT-like models terrible, because human and model psychology differ: a human's ideal solution has steps trivial to the model and others that are leaps it does not understand ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @25:32, @26:04). The model needs to practise for itself and learn what works for it; he calls imitation a good initializer for something agent-like ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @26:04, @26:39).

**RLHF is a weak form of RL.** He says RLHF is such a weak form of reinforcement learning that, he thinks, it does not even count, since its reward model is a vibe check: an AlphaGo trained by RLHF would ask people which of two boards they prefer and train against those labels ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @26:39, @27:13). A neural-net reward model is easy for the optimised model to overfit and hack in spurious ways, whereas AlphaGo had a clear objective function; he calls imitation learning super silly and RLHF a nice improvement that is still silly, and wants training where the model is in the loop with its own psychology ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @27:13, @27:46). Learning from a textbook involves exercises, note-taking and rephrasing, and he says nothing equivalent exists in LLMs yet: super early days ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @28:19).

**A staircase (No Priors, 2024-09-05).** He says current models are helping create the next generation of models, a staircase of improvement ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @18:05). **Note:** this is models helping build their successors, for instance through synthetic data, not the AlphaGo-style self-play of the sections above; no claim relation is set ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @18:05; [[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @38:46).

**AlphaGo and Move 37 (Deep Dive, 2025-02-05).** He does not use the word self-improvement in this lecture, but returns to AlphaGo: supervised learning on expert games tops out below the best humans, while RL plays the moves that lead to wins and beat Lee Sedol ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @2:42:41, @2:43:14, @2:43:46, @2:44:20). We are only starting to see hints of that diagram in LLMs for reasoning, he says, and need to go beyond imitating experts ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @2:44:20, @2:44:52). Move 37, which a human would play about 1 in 10,000 times, is his example of RL discovering a strategy unknown to humans; what the LLM equivalent would look like is unknown, perhaps new analogies, new thinking strategies or a language of its own rather than English ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @2:45:26, @2:46:00, @2:46:33, @2:47:05). He calls RL in LLMs primordial and nascent, in principle capable of a Move 37 in open-domain thinking ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @3:29:18, @3:29:50). On RLHF he repeats that its reward model can be gamed, so it is not RL in that sense; see [[rlhf-is-not-real-rl]] ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @3:05:54, @3:06:27). **Note:** his 2025 reasons why humans cannot write the model's best token sequences, and why it must find what works for itself, echo the Sequoia points above; a connection, not a link ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @25:32, @26:04; [[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @2:18:16, @2:19:55).

**What to borrow from animals (Animals vs Ghosts, 2025-10-01).** Reporting Richard Sutton's view that a human-data prior is off course, he likens it to AlphaZero, never shown human games, beating AlphaGo, which was initialized from them; the comparison is his paraphrase of Sutton's view ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). **Note:** the 2023 account above has AlphaGo's two stages, imitation then self-play; this is the same analogy taken one step further, from Sutton's side ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @38:14; [[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). He names ideas LLM agents might adapt from animals: intrinsic motivation, fun, curiosity, empowerment, multi-agent self-play and culture ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). On very long-horizon sparse rewards, which Dwarkesh raised with building a startup as the example, Sutton offered temporal-difference learning and future-reward discounting; he does not find that particularly compelling, thinks something else is going on, and that it is not reinforcement learning ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §Appendix). See [[bitter-lesson]] ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro).

**Terrible, but better than imitation (Dwarkesh, published 2025-10-17).** He says RL is terrible, because it broadcasts a single final reward across a whole trajectory, and just happens to beat the imitation that came before; see [[rl-is-terrible-but-better-than-imitation]] ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @41:33, @42:46). Process supervision with LLM judges is gameable, so RL against a judge works for maybe 10 or 20 steps, not hundreds; see [[llm-judges-are-gameable-rewards]] ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @46:34, @47:11). Asked what else could work, he points to reviewing solutions with synthetic examples and meta-learning, says he is only at the stage of reading abstracts, and has not seen it shown convincingly at frontier scale, though the labs are closed ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @49:46, @50:23). Reading a book is for a human a set of prompts for generating synthetic data, and he would like a pretraining stage that thinks material through; why the model cannot simply train on its own reflections is on [[model-collapse]] ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @51:00, @51:37). Culture and self-play, two multi-agent ideas no one has claimed, are [[llms-lack-culture-and-self-play]] ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @1:41:10, @1:41:47). On animals he says much of what looks like learning is maturation, that RL in animals is mostly motor tasks, and that humans don't really use RL for intelligence tasks like problem solving ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @10:04, @10:41).

**Practice needs a verifiable task (Verifiability, 2025-11-17).** He does not use the word self-improvement in the post: a task an AI can "practice" must be verifiable, with a resettable, efficient and rewardable environment, and tasks that are not verifiable fall back on generalisation or weaker means like imitation ([[blog-2025-11-17-verifiability]] §intro). See [[software-2-0-automates-what-you-can-verify]] ([[blog-2025-11-17-verifiability]] §intro).

**LLMs improving LLMs (Skill Issue, 2026-03-20).** He says that training GPT-2-size models is for him a little harness and playground, and that what he is fundamentally interested in is recursive self-improvement, the extent to which LLMs can improve LLMs; he thinks the frontier labs are all trying to do this, "roughly speaking" ([[talk-2026-03-20-skill-issue]] @17:32, @18:04). His [[autoresearch]] loop, left overnight on nanochat, came back with tunings he had missed ([[talk-2026-03-20-skill-issue]] @18:36). It needs an objective, easy-to-evaluate metric; see [[autoresearch-needs-an-objective-metric]] ([[talk-2026-03-20-skill-issue]] @24:02). **Note:** autoresearch is an agent editing a model's training code against a metric, not RL on the model's own behaviour, so it is not the AlphaGo-style step two this page began with ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @39:20; [[talk-2026-03-20-skill-issue]] @17:32).
