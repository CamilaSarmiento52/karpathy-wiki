# Technology diffusion
His observation that LLMs reverse the usual path of a new technology, from governments and corporations down to individuals: so far regular people have benefited disproportionately, while the impact on corporations and governments is more muted and lagging ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶1, ¶2).

## What he says
**The usual path.** Transformative technologies usually diffuse top-down, from government or military use through corporations to individuals; his examples are electricity, cryptography, computers, flight, the internet and GPS ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶1). He gives the reason as new technologies being scarce, capital-intensive and needing specialised expertise early on ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶1).

**The reversal.** He finds it unique and remarkable that LLMs reverse this, benefiting regular people disproportionately ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶2). He describes ChatGPT, in the post's figures, as the fastest-growing consumer application in history with 400 million weekly active users, used for writing, coding, translation, tutoring, summarisation, deep research and brainstorming ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶2). He calls it a major multiplier to an individual's power, with a very low barrier: cheap or free, fast, available on demand behind a URL or on a local machine, and in anyone's native language; as far as he can tell, "the average person has never experienced a technological unlock this dramatic, this fast." ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶2).

**Why organisations benefit less.** His first reason is the capability profile: LLMs give quasi-expert performance across many domains at once, versatile but shallow and fallible ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶3). An organisation already concentrates diverse experts, so LLMs make those experts more efficient and the organisation a bit better at what it could already do, while an individual, usually expert in at most one thing, can now do things they could not do before ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶3). His examples are vibe coding apps, approaching legal documents, grokking research papers, data analytics and generating marketing content, all at an adequate level without another expert ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶3). His second reason is that organisations face far greater complexity and coordination, such as integrations, legacy systems, security, privacy, compliance and legal risk, which is hard to fit into a context window and leaves a lower margin for error ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶4). His third is the inertia of large organisations: culture, precedent, turf wars, communication overhead, retraining and bureaucracy ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶4). He says he does not want to downplay LLMs' impact on organisations, but that for the moment and in aggregate they have changed individuals' lives significantly more ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶4).

**Money cannot buy better ChatGPT, for now.** Future diffusion, he says, depends on the "benefit distribution", which depends heavily on the dynamic range of performance as a function of capital spent ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶5). As of the post, frontier-grade performance is very accessible and cheap, and a marginal dollar beyond that does not buy better performance, reliability or autonomy: "Money can't buy better ChatGPT." ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶5). He asks whether that can last: train-time scaling, test-time scaling and model ensembles are forces increasing the dynamic range, while model distillation has decreased it ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶5). If money starts buying dramatically better models, he expects large organisations to buy more intelligence and an elite among individuals to split away from the rest ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶5).

**Not what science fiction predicted.** He says very few science-fiction works predicted this progression: the AI revolution was supposed to be a top-secret government project, not ChatGPT on a device already in everyone's pocket ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶6). He closes, after quoting William Gibson's line, that at least for now the future is "shockingly distributed", and that he loves it ([[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶6).

**The YC talk version (published 2025-06-19).** In the talk he says LLMs flip the direction of technology diffusion: early computers were about ballistics and military use, while with LLMs it is about how to boil an egg, which he says is a lot of his own use ([[youtube-2025-06-19-software-is-changing]] @12:45, @13:18). Corporations and governments, he says, are lagging behind everyone else's adoption ([[youtube-2025-06-19-software-is-changing]] @13:18). What is new and unprecedented is that LLMs are not in the hands of a few governments and corporations but of all of us ([[youtube-2025-06-19-software-is-changing]] @13:50). **Note:** in the talk he says he wrote about this property, without naming the post; the essay is dated 2025-04-07 and the talk published 2025-06-19 ([[youtube-2025-06-19-software-is-changing]] @12:45; [[blog-2025-04-07-power-to-the-people]] §intro ¶2).
