# Compute efficiency
How much useful model you get per unit of compute and energy. In the Sequoia interview he names a massive gap in the energetic efficiency of running these models as one of the open research problems ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @12:55).

## What he says
His brain runs on roughly 20 watts, while the supercomputers Jensen described at GTC are measured in megawatts; he does not know how much you need exactly, but thinks it safe to say we are probably off by a factor of a thousand to a million ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @12:55, @13:27). Part of the reason is that computers were not designed for this workload, which needs extremely high parallelism rather than data-dependent sequential computation; he calls Nvidia GPUs a good step in that direction ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @13:27).

His levers: adapt computer architecture to the new workload; keep lowering precision, which has come down from 64-bit doubles to a few bits or even 1.58 depending on the paper; and sparsity, since the brain is not always fully activated ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @13:59). The last lever is the von Neumann architecture, whose shuttling of data between memory and compute cores he calls kind of broken and unlike how the brain works ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @14:33). He says he is not a computer architect but expects a very exciting time in computer architecture ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @14:33). Asked later about changing the Transformer, he returns to precision, sparsity and co-design with the hardware ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @34:24, @34:57).

He also calls good dataset preparation and better algorithms compute-efficiency gains, alongside scale ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @09:38).

**Inside llm.c's training step (April 2024).** His first llm.c report profiles one GPT-2 training step on an A10: 76% of the time is in NVIDIA cutlass kernels, which he calls encouraging, with softmax, AdamW, classifier, layernorm and GELU kernels taking a few percent each ([[github-2024-04-22-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro). The step took 212.4 ms, about 48% in backward blocks, 26% in forward blocks, 23% in the classifier and 3% in the optimizer, and memory sat at a constant 8753 MiB, which he credits to heavy optimisation by @ngc92 ([[github-2024-04-22-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro, §intro (peak memory)). Next on his list was faster attention through kernel improvement or fusion, for example only computing lower-triangular matmuls ([[github-2024-04-22-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro ("big stones" ongoing work)). All the figures are his measurements on his hardware ([[github-2024-04-22-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro).

**What made llm.c fast (May 2024).** His day-24 report lists the changes behind the speedup: bfloat16 mixed-precision training with a master copy of the parameters in fp32; kernel optimisations, including a fused classifier that he says improves algorithmically on what the PyTorch compiler does by not materialising normalised logits and evaluating the loss only at the label's index; flash attention, currently cuDNN's; and Packed128, a structure a bit like float4 that supports mixed precision and forces 128-bit load and store instructions to maximise memory bandwidth ([[github-2024-05-03-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro (Single GPU training)). Memory fell by deleting a large amount of unneeded memory for backward-pass gradients and by being careful with allocation, especially in the fused classifier ([[github-2024-05-03-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro (Single GPU training), §intro (Peak memory)). A first multi-GPU version uses MPI and NCCL, with a ZeRO stage 1 (optimizer state sharding) pull request about to merge ([[github-2024-05-03-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro (Multi-GPU training)); by late May, `-z 1` turns ZeRO-1 on, which he says saves GPU memory ([[github-2024-05-24-llmc-pytorch-cross-checks]] §multiGPU). In his profile at batch 24 a step takes 302.5 ms, with `ampere_bf16` kernels about 55% of the time and cuDNN attention about 9% ([[github-2024-05-03-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro (Runtime, DRAM traffic, instructions)). cuDNN flash attention gives large speedups and memory savings but raises compile time to about 1m24s, so he wants to delete that dependency ([[github-2024-05-03-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro (Latency)).

**Utilisation and budget (llm.c GPT-2, 2024-05-28).** MFU, model flops utilisation, counts the FLOPs done, divides by time and compares with the A100's claimed 312 TFLOPS, a peak he says is very hard to reach because training is memory-bound and the tensor cores cannot be fed ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Training)). His measurements: roughly half the theoretical peak, about 178K tokens/s, on an A100 40GB PCIe, and about 60% on the A100 80GB SXM with more memory bandwidth and power; the number is in A100 fp16 units and meaningless on other GPUs ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Training)). `-r 1` recomputes the GELU activations, slightly slower but saving enough memory to raise the batch size for a net throughput gain; ZeRO-1 is a no-brainer on more than one GPU and a no-op on one; out of memory, he turns on `-r 1`, halves the batch until it runs, then tries `-r 0` again ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Args guide)). He sizes runs with the 6ND approximation: 124M on 10B tokens is about 7e18 FLOPs and 350M on about 30B about 7e19, roughly 10X, matching the 10X in cost from about $20 to about $200 ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (350M model)).

**Budget for 774M (llm.c, 2024-06-12).** His config comment for the 774M run, planning arithmetic written before it: 6 × 774e6 × 150e9 ≈ 7e20 FLOPs, ten times the 350M; 286,102 steps of 524,288 tokens at about 1.7 s per step on 8×A100 80GB SXM at $14/hour, about 135 hours, about 5.6 days and about $2,000, ten times the 124M ([[github-2024-06-12-llmc-gpt-2-774m]] §intro). The reported run took about six days ([[github-2024-06-12-llmc-gpt-2-774m]] §intro).

**GPT-2 1.6B in 24 hours (llm.c, 2024-07-11).** He credits five years of improvements in compute (H100s), software (CUDA, cuBLAS, cuDNN, FlashAttention) and data (FineWeb-Edu) for reproducing the 1558M GPT-2, an involved team project in 2019, on one 8×H100 node in 24 hours for $672 ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro). Each step takes about 2.75 s over about 1M tokens, 32,000 steps and 33.6B tokens in all, at an MFU around 50%, which he calls quite efficient; the first log lines show 42.6–47.5% bf16 MFU ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Training)). His estimates for other setups: about 8 days on one GPU, about 12 hours on 16 GPUs across nodes, and perhaps about 30 minutes on a 512-GPU H100 cluster at $2,300/hour with a larger total batch (about 8M) and some tuning, which he has not tried ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Training), §intro (Multi-node training)). The most GPUs he has seen llm.c trained on is about 500; his own largest run was 16 H100s across two nodes ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Multi-node training)).

**His memory guide.** If the model does not fit, first lower the micro-batch size in nice numbers, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1; then turn on recompute, `-r 1` very slightly slower with a huge memory saving or `-r 2` slightly slower with a smaller one; then drop the fp32 master weights with `-w 0`, which has empirically seemed fine, likely thanks to stochastic rounding; and only last shorten the sequence, which makes the model worse ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Memory guide)). Recompute trades compute for memory, recomputing the GELU (and with `-r 2` the layernorms) in the backward pass instead of caching it ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Args guide)). Memory matters, he says, because it lets you raise the batch size, which goes a bit faster ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (PyTorch comparison)).

**Quantization (llama2.c).** The default `run.c` keeps the whole forward pass in fp32, which he calls very easy to understand as reference code but which makes checkpoints large, 4 bytes per weight, and the forward pass relatively slow ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §int8 quantization). Quantizing parameters to lower precision is, he says, a very common inference optimisation that gives up a little correctness for smaller checkpoints and faster forward passes; LLMs empirically tolerate 4-bit or lower, but he uses int8 as a "safe" setting ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §int8 quantization). Only weights in matmuls are quantized; other parameters, especially the RMSNorm scale and bias, stay float32 because those layers are very sensitive ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §int8 quantization). Quantizing weights alone and dequantizing for float32 inference is fine if you only want smaller files; llama2.c also quantizes activations at runtime so the matmuls run in pure integer arithmetic, which is where the speedups come from, using what llama.cpp calls Q8_0 ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §int8 quantization). His measurement on Llama 2 7B: 26GB in fp32 against 6.7GB in int8, and 4.6 against 14 tokens/s, a 3X speedup with a 4X smaller checkpoint, at silently very slightly lower quality ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §int8 quantization). On CPUs he also points to compile flags (`-Ofast`, `-march=native`) and OpenMP, warning that more threads is not always better and is usually U-shaped ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §performance).

**The GPT-2 lecture's speed-up sequence (2024-06-09).** He starts from what the hardware offers: an eight-GPU A100 SXM 80GB box rented from Lambda Labs, which he discloses sponsors his work ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:22:39, @1:23:11). PyTorch defaults to float32 for parameters and activations, which he calls far more precision than deep learning needs ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:23:43, @1:24:17). From the A100 datasheet he reads about 19.5 TFLOPS in FP32, about 8× that in TF32 and about 16× (312 TFLOPS) in FP16/BF16; the sparsity figures do not apply, and INT8 is for inference because training needs floats ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:24:50, @1:25:25, @1:25:58, @1:26:31). A tensor core is an instruction for a small 4×4 matrix multiply, nearly all of the Transformer's work is matrix multiplies, and at 124M the 768-to-50,257 classifier dominates ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:28:12, @1:28:45, @1:29:21). His baseline with batch 16 and sequence 1,024 was about 1,000 ms per step, and he reports tokens per second as the objective throughput measure ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:34:24, @1:36:09, @1:36:41). The steps, all his measurements on one A100:

- **TF32.** TF32 keeps FP32's sign and 8 exponent bits but truncates the mantissa inside the instruction, invisibly to the code, and empirically he cannot tell the difference ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:31:01, @1:31:36, @1:32:09). One line, `set_float32_matmul_precision('high')`, took the step from about 1,000 to about 300 ms, about 3× rather than 8×, because the workload is still memory-bound on FP32 data ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:36:41, @1:38:24, @1:38:59).
- **bfloat16 autocast.** BF16 keeps FP32's exponent range with less mantissa; FP16 reduces range and needs gradient scalers, which BF16, available from Ampere, avoids ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:40:07, @1:40:40, @1:41:12, @1:41:45, @1:42:18). Following PyTorch's guidance he wraps only the forward pass and loss in `torch.autocast` to bfloat16 ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:42:51, @1:43:25, @1:43:58). Matmuls convert while softmax, layer norm and the loss may stay in float32, and he is not sure exactly what autocast converts ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:44:32, @1:45:06, @1:45:41, @1:46:15, @1:47:23). The step went from about 333 to 300 ms, about 50,000 to 55,000 tokens per second; he accepts slightly less precision because you can train longer to make up for it ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:46:51, @1:47:57).
- **torch.compile.** A compiler for neural networks, like GCC for C/C++, it took the step from 300 to 129 ms, about 2.3× from one line, and he says there is no real good reason not to use it unless debugging ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:48:29, @1:49:01, @1:49:35). It removes the Python interpreter from the forward pass and fuses kernels: in his GELU example, elementwise operations make repeated round trips between GPU memory (HBM) and the chip, which fusion replaces with one ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:50:43, @1:51:15, @1:53:28, @1:54:36, @1:55:09). On-chip memory is very fast but only tens of megabytes; HBM is tens of gigabytes but expensive to reach ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:57:23, @1:58:28, @1:59:03). The same video records its cost: it broke his sampling and HellaSwag code, so he turned it off there ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:27:09, @3:38:55).
- **Flash attention.** Flash attention (Stanford, 2022) is a kernel fusion torch.compile cannot find because it needs an algorithmic rewrite; it does more FLOPs, but the paper cites up to 7.6× because it never materialises the T×T attention matrix in HBM, and it rests on an online-softmax trick from a 2018 NVIDIA paper ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:00:09, @2:01:13, @2:01:48, @2:02:23, @2:02:57, @2:03:32). Swapping four attention lines for `F.scaled_dot_product_attention` took the step from about 130 to 96 ms with the same loss ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:05:15, @2:05:49, @2:06:21).
- **Nice numbers.** He calls the next one simultaneously the dumbest and the most brilliant optimisation: numbers with many factors of two, because CUDA kernels work in power-of-two blocks and handle leftovers with slow boundary kernels, so he scans the code for ugly numbers such as 50,257 or GPT-2 XL's 25 heads ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:06:54, @2:07:28, @2:08:01, @2:08:36). Padding the vocabulary to 50,304 adds FLOPs but took the step from 96.5 to 93 ms; the extra tokens are never used and are driven to zero probability ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:09:10, @2:10:51, @2:11:25, @2:12:33, @2:13:08, @2:13:42). On PyTorch nightly that was 4%; on 2.3.1 or earlier he says it would be about 30% ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:14:16). He also keeps batch sizes nice ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:35:32, @3:22:06).
- **Overall.** About 1,000 to about 93 ms per step, roughly 11× ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:14:49). Fused AdamW, which updates all parameters in one kernel and which he does not know why is not the default, took it from 93 to 90 ms ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:31:41, @2:32:13, @2:33:20).

**The batch-size ramp.** He skips GPT-3's gradual batch-size increase because it complicates the arithmetic, and his understanding is that it is a systems and speed improvement rather than an algorithmic one: early in training the model mostly learns which tokens appear, so per-example gradients are highly correlated and a small batch gives nearly the same gradient, while later they decorrelate ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:26:05, @2:26:38, @2:27:13, @2:27:46).

**Distributed data parallel.** DDP launches one process per GPU with `torchrun`; each knows its rank, local rank and world size, works on different data, and gradients are averaged ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:47:27, @2:48:00, @2:48:34, @2:49:07). Its backward calls all-reduce and can overlap communication with the backward pass; with gradient accumulation he syncs only on the last micro-step by toggling `require_backward_grad_sync` directly instead of using `no_sync`, which he calls a naughty thing to do ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:00:59, @3:01:34, @3:02:40, @3:03:14, @3:03:48, @3:04:23). The logged loss must be all-reduced too, and the data loader strides by rank ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:57:35, @2:58:10, @3:05:31, @3:06:38). On eight GPUs he reached about 1.5M tokens per second ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:07:44, @3:21:29). On FineWeb-Edu at micro-batch 64 per GPU no accumulation was needed: a step took about 330 ms, and he projected the 19,073-step run at about 1.7 hours ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:20:45, @3:21:29, @3:22:06). Comparing it with llm.c on one GPU, he measured about 223,000 tokens per second for the CUDA code against about 185,000 for PyTorch, with identical losses and norms, while not being sure he squeezed the PyTorch side fully ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:58:46, @3:59:19).

**In the LLM101n syllabus (2024-08-01).** His planned course gives three "Need for Speed" chapters to device (CPU, GPU), precision (mixed precision, fp16, bf16, fp8) and distributed training (DDP, ZeRO), plus inference chapters on the kv-cache and quantization; these are chapter titles, not explanations ([[github-2024-08-01-llm101n-readme]] §intro (Syllabus)).

**Library kernels and their cost (llm.c README, 2024-09-24).** To be fast enough to reproduce the GPT-2 (1.6B) run, llm.c incorporates the fastest kernels, including cuBLAS, cuBLASLt, CUTLASS and cuDNN; he says this also serves education by setting an expert upper bound and unit of measurement for hand-written kernels, and lets users choose a fast run or "drag and drop" their own kernels ([[github-2024-09-24-llmc-readme]] §repo). cuDNN flash attention, used since 2024-05-01, is off by default because it raises compile time from a few seconds to about a minute and the path is very new ([[github-2024-09-24-llmc-readme]] §tutorial). Multi-GPU training uses MPI and NCCL, and multi-node offers three NCCL initialisation methods for different environments ([[github-2024-09-24-llmc-readme]] §multi-GPU training, §multi-node training).

**The optimisation list (talk outline, 2024-10-02).** His outline of the llm.c talk lists, after the fp32 CUDA port, cuBLAS matmuls, cuDNN flash attention, bfloat16 mixed precision, kernel fusions, Packed128, stochastic rounding, full determinism, multi-GPU with NCCL and a sharded optimizer, and multi-node with MPI, a file system or sockets; the post lists them without explanation ([[x-2024-10-02-gpu-mode-talk]]).

**How the optimisations work (CUDA MODE keynote, published 2024-10-03).** Matmuls moved from hand-rolled fp32 to cuBLAS, and attention to cuDNN's flash attention rather than a kernel of their own ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @13:44, @14:17). Mixed precision means deciding for every parameter and activation tensor which is float32 and which bfloat16 and converting between them ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @14:17). Further work included kernel fusions, recompute settings that redo part of the forward pass during the backward, and contributor work minimising backward-pass memory ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @14:50). Packed128 is a data structure that, in their experience, forces the compiler to use 128-bit load and store instructions it otherwise often does not, found by reading the SASS assembly for a loop ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @14:50, @15:25). Full determinism is hard because some kernels cannot use atomics; the encoder backward is trivial with atomics and non-trivial without ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @15:58, @16:31). Across GPUs they use NCCL and a sharded optimizer state (ZeRO-1) that spreads AdamW's large float buffers to lower memory per GPU ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @16:31, @17:05). On one node llm.c reaches almost 50% MFU, which he calls quite good ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @18:44).

**GPT-2 cost then and now (Deep Dive, 2025-02-05).** Training GPT-2 was estimated at about $40,000 in 2019; his llm.c reproduction took about a day and $600, and he thinks about $100 is possible now ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @33:01, @33:36). He attributes the fall to better datasets and filtering and, above all, to faster hardware and better software for squeezing speed out of it ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @33:36, @34:10). He rents an 8×H100 node from Lambda at about $3 per GPU per hour; GPUs suit training because the matrix multiplications are highly parallel ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @39:06, @39:42, @40:16). Smaller distilled models at lower precision can run locally, for example in LM Studio on a MacBook ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @3:19:45, @3:20:20, @3:20:53).
