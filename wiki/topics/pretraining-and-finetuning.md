# Pretraining and finetuning
Training ChatGPT, as he describes it in January 2023, has roughly two stages: pretraining, which trains a decoder-only Transformer on a large chunk of the internet to babble text, and finetuning, which aligns the result into an assistant ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:48:42, @1:49:14, @1:52:36).

## What he says
Pretraining is very similar to the lecture's tiny baby pretraining step ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:48:42, @1:49:14). His Shakespeare model has about 10 million parameters trained on about 1 million characters, roughly 300,000 tokens in OpenAI's vocabulary ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:49:14, @1:49:47). GPT-3's largest model has 175 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens, about a millionfold more data, and that is not even large by today's standards; training at that scale is a massive infrastructure challenge with thousands of GPUs ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:50:22, @1:50:56). He says GPT-3's architecture is nearly identical to what the lecture implemented, and its hyperparameters (layers, n_embd, heads, head size, batch size, learning rate) should be recognisable ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:50:22, @1:50:56). Models like GPT-3 are between 10,000 and 1 million times bigger depending on how you count ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:55:22).

After pretraining you get a document completer that babbles internet, may answer questions with more questions, and is unaligned ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:51:29, @1:52:02). The finetuning stage aligns it to be an assistant in roughly three steps: supervised finetuning on question-answer documents, perhaps thousands of examples; raters ranking responses to train a reward model; and PPO, a policy-gradient reinforcement-learning optimiser, finetuning the sampling policy to score high reward ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:52:36, @1:53:08, @1:53:43). Very large models are very sample efficient during finetuning ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:53:08). Much of this data is internal to OpenAI and much harder to replicate; nanoGPT focuses on pretraining ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:54:16). Anything that is not a plain document completer, such as task performance, alignment or sentiment, requires further finetuning stages, simple supervised finetuning or, as in ChatGPT, a reward model with rounds of PPO ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:55:22, @1:55:55).

**Four stages (State of GPT, May 2023).** At Microsoft Build he calls the recipe emerging, very new and still rapidly evolving, with four stages that follow each other serially: pretraining, supervised finetuning, reward modeling and reinforcement learning, each with its own dataset, objective and resulting model ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @00:35, @01:07). Pretraining is about 99 percent of the training compute, with internet-scale data, thousands of GPUs and possibly months; the other three stages use a few GPUs for hours or days ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @01:07, @01:41). His example data mixture is LLaMA's: CommonCrawl, C4 and higher-quality sets such as GitHub, Wikipedia and books, sampled in given proportions ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @01:41, @02:16). Documents are packed into B×T rows separated by end-of-text tokens, and at every position the Transformer predicts the next token, the actual next token supervising the weights ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @05:06, @05:41, @06:45). The plot to watch is the loss over time ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @07:48).

He says LLaMA 65B is significantly more powerful than GPT-3 175B because it trained on 1.4 trillion tokens instead of 300 billion, so a model's power should not be judged by its parameter count ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @03:56). His figures for Meta's run are about 2,000 GPUs for about 21 days, roughly several million dollars ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @04:31). The field noticed that pretraining learns powerful general representations that can be finetuned for downstream tasks with few examples, because predicting the next token forces the model to multitask; around GPT-2 people found that prompting with fake documents could work even better, which he says began an era of prompting over finetuning ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @07:48, @08:23, @08:56, @09:31).

Supervised finetuning collects small, high-quality datasets of prompts and ideal responses written by contractors, typically tens of thousands, and is still language modeling with only the training set changed ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @11:43). The contractors follow long labelling documentation asking them to be helpful, truthful and harmless ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @12:15, @12:48). Reward modeling has the SFT model write several completions per prompt, people rank them, and a Transformer is trained to predict a reward at a special readout token that agrees with the rankings ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @13:23, @13:57, @14:31). The reward model is not deployable as an assistant but can score any completion ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @15:04). In the RL stage the model, initialised from SFT, samples completions, the frozen reward model scores them, and the language-modeling loss on the sampled tokens is weighted by the reward ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @15:35, @16:08). In his account RLHF is reward modeling plus reinforcement learning, and ChatGPT is an RLHF model ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @12:48, @16:43).

**Base models and assistant models.** He distinguishes three kinds of model, base, SFT and RLHF; ChatGPT is RLHF and Vicuna-13B is SFT ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @16:43). The GPT-4 base model was never released; the GPT-4 served over the API is an assistant model ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @09:31, @10:05). A few-shot prompt laid out as a human–assistant exchange can make a base model act as an assistant, but not very reliably ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @10:36, @11:09). On the Berkeley Elo ranking he puts GPT-4 best by far, then Claude and GPT-3.5; he says these first three are RLHF models and the rest, to his knowledge, SFT models ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @19:29, @20:02).

**RLHF models lose entropy.** He says RLHF models are not strictly an improvement on base models in some cases: they lose some entropy and give peakier samples with lower variation ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @18:21). So when you have n things and want more like them, he still prefers a base model; his example completes seven Pokémon names into many more, which he checked were fictitious ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @18:55, @19:29).

**Finetuning in practice.** Finetuning is becoming more accessible through parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA, which train small pieces and keep most of the model clamped, allowing low-precision inference for the frozen parts ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @35:00, @35:33, @36:07). It still needs more technical expertise, contractors or synthetic-data pipelines, and slows iteration; SFT is achievable, but he calls RLHF research territory, unstable and not beginner-friendly, and would not advise rolling your own ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @36:07, @36:40).

**Lossy compression of the internet (Intro to LLMs, November 2023).** In his general-audience talk he takes Llama 2 70B as the example because Meta released the weights, architecture and paper, unlike ChatGPT, which you only use through a web interface ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @00:33, @01:06). The architecture and forward pass are algorithmically understood and open; the magic, and the cost, is in obtaining the parameters ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @03:52). His figures for Llama 2 70B are roughly 10 TB of crawled internet text, about 6,000 GPUs for about 12 days and about $2 million ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @04:25, @04:57). He calls these rookie numbers next to frontier models, which are off by a factor of 10 or more and cost tens or hundreds of millions of dollars to train, whereas running a trained network is fairly cheap ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @06:02, @06:37). The network predicts the next word, as in "cat sat on a" followed by "mat" at 97 percent, and because prediction and compression are closely related he calls training a compression of the internet ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @06:37, @07:10, @07:43). Predicting the next word of a Wikipedia page about Ruth Handler requires knowing who she was and what she did, so the objective forces the network to learn a lot about the world ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @07:43, @08:16, @08:50). Sampled word by word, the network "dreams" internet documents: Java code, an Amazon product page with a made-up ISBN, a Wikipedia-like article about a fish that is roughly correct but not verbatim, so you never know which parts are memorized and which hallucinated ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @08:50, @09:25, @09:58, @10:30, @11:02).

**Finetuning into an assistant.** Pretraining yields internet document generators; finetuning keeps the same next-word objective but swaps the dataset for conversations written by people hired with labeling instructions, giving an assistant model ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @14:21, @14:55). Pretraining data is large but low quality; finetuning prefers quality over quantity, for example 100,000 high-quality conversations ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @16:04). He calls it remarkable, empirical and not fully understood that the finetuned model changes its format to a helpful assistant while still using the knowledge from pretraining; roughly, pretraining is about knowledge and finetuning about alignment ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @17:11). Pretraining costs millions of dollars and happens inside companies maybe once a year, while finetuning is much cheaper, perhaps a day, so companies iterate on it weekly or daily ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @17:45, @18:18, @18:50, @19:58). The finetuning loop is to evaluate, deploy, monitor misbehaviours, have a person write the correct response, add it to the training data and repeat ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @19:24). Meta's Llama 2 release includes both base and assistant models; the base model is not directly usable but lets you do your own finetuning ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @19:58, @20:30).

An optional stage three uses comparison labels, because comparing candidate answers is easier than writing one, for example picking the best haiku; at OpenAI this is called reinforcement learning from human feedback ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @21:03, @21:36, @22:09). The labeling instructions, from the InstructGPT paper, ask for helpful, truthful and harmless responses and can run to tens or hundreds of pages ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @22:09). Labeling is increasingly a human–machine collaboration, with models sampling answers, checking work or creating comparisons and people in oversight, a slider he says is moving to the right as models improve ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @22:42, @23:15). Chatbot Arena, from a Berkeley team, ranks models by Elo from blind pairwise votes ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @23:15, @23:47).

**Special tokens in finetuning (tokenizer lecture, February 2024).** `<|endoftext|>` delimits documents in the pretraining stream ([[youtube-2024-02-20-gpt-tokenizer]] @1:19:34, @1:20:07). Finetuning a base model into a chat model introduces many new special tokens to hold the structure of a conversation between user and assistant, which requires small model surgery on the embedding and output layers, a very common operation ([[youtube-2024-02-20-gpt-tokenizer]] @1:21:49, @1:24:37, @1:25:09). His trailing-space demonstration uses `gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct`, which he describes as a completion model closer to a base model ([[youtube-2024-02-20-gpt-tokenizer]] @1:58:52).

**Finetuning regresses, components compose (Sequoia, 2024-03-26).** The more you finetune a model, the more it regresses on everything else; adding a capability without that wants training on a mixture of the old and new data, which needs the dataset and training loop, not just the weights ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @08:32). Asked about model composability, he says he sees papers on model merges but does not know that anything has really stuck ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @23:21). Neural nets are less composable than traditional code, but they do compose through pretraining: it is very common to pretrain components separately, plug them in and finetune through the whole, for example adding images to a ChatGPT-like system ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @23:53, @24:26). The AlphaGo and RLHF points from the same interview are on [[llm-self-improvement]] ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @24:59).

**Note:** the January lecture's three finetuning steps (SFT, reward model, PPO) are the same recipe this talk lays out as four stages with the last two named RLHF; more detail, not a change of view ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:52:36; [[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @01:07).

**Pretraining only (llm.c, 2024-05-28).** llm.c is currently pretraining only, not chat finetuning, so you cannot chat with its GPT-2 reproduction; without a GPU you can still finetune OpenAI's GPT-2 weights on other data such as TinyShakespeare or TinyStories with the more rudimentary `train_gpt2.c` ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (FAQ)).

**Pretraining data: WebText to FineWeb-Edu (GPT-2 lecture, 2024-06-09).** Per the GPT-2 paper, WebText was built from Reddit outbound links with at least 3 karma, 45M links and 40 GB of text; it was never released, and OpenWebText attempts to reproduce it ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:09:57, @3:10:32). He says Common Crawl is extremely noisy, "much worse than you think", mostly ad spam, tables and stock tickers, so people filter it heavily and train on curated mixtures ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:11:05, @3:11:38). GPT-3's mixture, never released, added WebText, books and Wikipedia to Common Crawl; he names RedPajama and its cleaned, deduplicated SlimPajama subset as representative mixtures ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:11:38, @3:12:12). FineWeb filters Common Crawl to 15T tokens, and FineWeb-Edu is a 1.3T educational and a 5.4T high-educational subset, Hugging Face's figures as he reads them; he believes Llama 3 70B judged which content is educational ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:12:12, @3:12:45, @3:14:58).

He uses FineWeb-Edu's 10B-token sample because in his previous experiments that suffices to get close to GPT-2 performance, and shards it into 100 numpy files of 100M tokens, the first being validation ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:13:17, @3:13:50, @3:16:39, @3:17:14, @3:17:48). He suspects the sample was not properly shuffled, giving his loss curve a periodicity he does not fully understand ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:46:29, @3:47:04). For multi-epoch runs, he says, one should permute the documents within each shard every epoch, and possibly the shards, because document order is a spurious correlation ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:49:49, @3:50:22). Among possible reasons his model beat GPT-2 on fewer tokens he names FineWeb-Edu being English-only with little math and code, and today's data getting more care over deduplication and quality filtering than early WebText ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:45:24, @3:48:11).

**SFT is a dataset swap (2024-06-09).** Everything the lecture builds is pretraining only: the model dreams documents and you cannot talk to it like ChatGPT ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:55:21). Supervised finetuning, he says, is not much more complicated: swap in a conversational dataset with a user-assistant structure and continue training, filling in the user tokens and sampling the assistant tokens ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @3:55:54).

The build-nanogpt README says the same on 2024-06-17: the repo covers only pretraining, and the finetuning that comes after is quite simple conceptually, since SFT is just swapping out the dataset and continuing the training; it will be covered later ([[github-2024-06-17-build-nanogpt-readme]] §intro). **Note:** this is about conceptual simplicity; the January 2023 lecture's point that finetuning is much harder to replicate concerns OpenAI's internal data, so the two do not conflict ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:54:16; [[github-2024-06-17-build-nanogpt-readme]] §intro).

In the 2024-07-11 llm.c post the 1.6B model is again pretraining only, so you cannot chat with it, and SFT and RLHF finetuning are on the roadmap ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (FAQ), §intro (Coming up)). Finetuning OpenAI's GPT-2 for 1,000 steps at LR 1e-4 quickly adapts it to the new data's statistics ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Evals)).

**In the LLM101n syllabus (2024-08-01).** The planned course splits finetuning into two chapters, SFT (PEFT, LoRA, chat) and RL (RLHF, PPO, DPO); these are chapter headings only ([[github-2024-08-01-llm101n-readme]] §intro (Syllabus)).

**Data as the bottleneck (No Priors, 2024-09-05).** Internet data, he says, is not the data you want for a Transformer but a nearest neighbour that gets you surprisingly far ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @17:33). What you want is the inner thought monologue of a brain solving problems; with a billion such trajectories, roughly speaking, AGI would be here ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @17:33). Internet data happens to contain enough reasoning traces and knowledge, and the Transformer makes it work ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @17:33, @18:05). A lot of activity is refactoring the dataset into inner-monologue formats, with a lot of synthetic data generation, which must keep its entropy ([[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @18:05, @19:10). **Note:** the loss of diversity he describes in synthetic data is akin to the RLHF entropy loss above; a connection, not a claim relation ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @18:21; [[talk-2024-09-05-no-priors-ep-80]] @18:38).

**The pipeline for a general audience (Deep Dive, 2025-02-05).** His example of processed internet data is Hugging Face's FineWeb, which he says every major provider has some internal equivalent of; it ends up about 44 TB and about 15 trillion tokens ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @01:09, @02:14, @14:40). Most of it comes from Common Crawl, which he says had indexed 2.7 billion web pages as of 2024 ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @02:48). The steps he walks through are URL block-listing, text extraction from HTML, language filtering (FineWeb keeps pages that are over 65% English), deduplication and removal of personal information ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @03:21, @03:54, @04:28, @05:35). Language filtering is a design decision: filter out Spanish and the model will be weak at Spanish ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @05:02). Training samples windows of tokens, up to some maximum such as 8,000, and nudges the network so the actual next token gets higher probability, for all tokens in parallel in large batches ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @15:44, @18:32, @19:38).

The result is a base model, a token simulator of internet text and not by itself an assistant; few companies release base models ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @43:04, @43:38). Llama 3, his example, is a 405-billion-parameter model trained on 15 trillion tokens, and Meta released both base and instruct versions ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @45:54, @46:30). Post-training is computationally much cheaper: pretraining can take about three months on thousands of computers, post-training something like three hours ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:00:31, @1:03:51). It keeps the same algorithm and swaps the internet documents for a dataset of conversations, which programs the assistant implicitly, by example ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:02:08, @1:03:16, @1:04:26). Conversations are encoded as one-dimensional token sequences with new special tokens, such as GPT-4o's im_start, that are trained only in post-training; formats differ by provider, which he calls a little bit of a wild west ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:06:03, @1:06:39, @1:07:10). At inference the server appends the assistant's start tokens and samples the reply ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:08:51, @1:09:24).

The InstructGPT paper (OpenAI, 2022), as he describes it, used contractors from Upwork or Scale AI who wrote prompts and ideal responses following labeling instructions, helpful, truthful and harmless, usually hundreds of pages long ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:10:29, @1:11:35, @1:12:08). Modern SFT datasets such as UltraChat are largely synthetic, with LLMs drafting and humans editing, and run to millions of conversations ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:14:58, @1:15:29, @1:16:02). So the assistant's answer is, roughly, a simulation of a labeler; see [[chatgpt-answers-simulate-a-human-data-labeler]] ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @1:18:17).

He maps the three stages onto a textbook: exposition is pretraining, worked solutions are SFT, and practice problems with answers in the key are RL ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @2:11:38, @2:12:13, @2:13:19, @2:26:32). At companies like OpenAI each stage is a separate team handing the model on ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @2:10:31). Pretraining and SFT are standard everywhere, he says, while RL is early and not standard ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @2:27:37); the RL stage is on [[rlvr]], and his view that RLHF is not real RL on [[rlhf-is-not-real-rl]] ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @3:05:54). His recap: pretraining is knowledge acquisition, and the personality comes in SFT ([[youtube-2025-02-05-deep-dive-into-llms]] @3:23:06, @3:23:39).

**A zip file with a smiley face (How I use LLMs, 2025-02-27).** He describes pretraining as compressing the internet into a lossy, probabilistic zip file, about one terabyte for roughly a trillion parameters, that predicts the next token ([[youtube-2025-02-27-how-i-use-llms]] @07:55, @08:28, @09:00). It costs tens of millions of dollars and months, so it is done rarely and models have a knowledge cutoff ([[youtube-2025-02-27-how-i-use-llms]] @09:33, @10:09). Post-training is attaching a smiley face to the zip file, swapping in human-built conversations so it takes on an assistant persona ([[youtube-2025-02-27-how-i-use-llms]] @10:41). His suggested self-introduction for ChatGPT: a one-terabyte zip file whose knowledge comes from reading the internet about six months ago, remembered vaguely, with a personality programmed by example by human labelers ([[youtube-2025-02-27-how-i-use-llms]] @12:20). Things mentioned often on the internet are remembered better than rare ones ([[youtube-2025-02-27-how-i-use-llms]] @12:52).

**Human input at every stage, and a crappy evolution (Animals vs Ghosts, 2025-10-01).** Frontier LLMs, he writes, have human input at every stage: pretraining data is all human text, finetuning data is human and curated, and the RL environment mixture is tuned by engineers ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). He casts pretraining as a practical way to gather soft constraints over billions of parameters, one candidate answer to the cold-start problem, followed by RL finetuning that he says frontier labs now do pervasively; see [[pretraining-is-our-crappy-evolution]] ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Richard Sutton, as he reports him, rejects even pretraining-as-prior followed by RL as tainted by human bias ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §intro). Supervised learning with actions is the default mode for LLMs in pretraining and SFT, he notes, and nearly absent in animals; see [[bitter-lesson]] ([[blog-2025-10-01-animals-vs-ghosts]] §Appendix).

**Midtraining (nanochat launch post, 2025-10-13).** In his own pipeline pretraining is the most computationally heavy part, training the LLM to compress internet web text by predicting the next token, where it gains a lot of knowledge about the world ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Pretraining). The data is FineWeb-EDU, which he re-packaged into simple shuffled parquet shards because `datasets.load_dataset()` is too heavy and obscures simple logic; d20 needs 240 shards (setting), about 24GB to download (estimate) ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Train the tokenizer). The base model is "a fancy autocomplete": his prompts show it knows Paris and that Saturday follows Friday, but not the colour of the sky or simple maths ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Pretraining).

Midtraining comes next: finetuning on smol-SmolTalk conversations, algorithmically identical to pretraining, which teaches the multi-turn special tokens, adapts the model to the conversation distribution, teaches multiple choice with 100K MMLU auxiliary_train rows and teaches Python-interpreter tool use; he says it could also target things like context-length extension, not explored yet ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Midtraining). The default mixture is 460K SmolTalk, 100K MMLU and 8K GSM8K rows, 568K in all (setting), and it takes about 8 minutes against about 3 hours of pretraining (measured) ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Midtraining). **Note:** the four-stage recipe in State of GPT has no stage between pretraining and SFT; midtraining is new to the wiki here ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @01:07; [[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Midtraining).

SFT is another round on conversations, where ideally you cherry-pick the best data and do safety training such as refusals; it pads rows to mimic the test-time format instead of concatenating them, fixing a domain mismatch for a small "tightening the screws" boost, in about 7 minutes (measured) ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Supervised Finetuning (SFT)). The RL stage is commented out by default: he says RLHF gains a few percent and mitigates sampling-loop failures such as hallucinations and infinite loops, not major at this scale, and GSM8K's objective reward lets a simplified GRPO hill-climb it; see [[rlvr]] ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Reinforcement Learning (RL)). **Note:** the llm.c posts were pretraining only, with SFT and RLHF on the roadmap; this post is his own full pipeline ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Coming up); [[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §intro).

**New abilities through midtraining and SFT (strawberry guide, 2025-10-24).** He adds a capability to nanochat, as usual, through synthetic data generation during midtraining and SFT: a task generates conversations with the correct answer attached, which nanochat imitates once finetuned on them; see [[teach-a-capability-with-synthetic-tasks]] ([[github-2025-10-24-nanochat-strawberry-guide]] §intro). He calls reasoning examples shown in SFT the "prior" that RL can later build on ([[github-2025-10-24-nanochat-strawberry-guide]] §Reasoning structure).

**SFT on d34 (2025-11-20).** On d34, SFT barely moves ChatCORE (0.4087 to 0.4100) and lowers HumanEval (0.1159 to 0.0488) (measured), which he attributes mostly to noise and expects better tuning could recover; on GSM8K, midtraining shows good lift and SFT "botches it a bit again" in his words ([[github-2025-11-20-nanochat-d34-model]] §intro). The numbers are on [[llm-evaluation]] ([[github-2025-11-20-nanochat-d34-model]] §intro).

**Imitation as the primordial behaviour (The space of minds, 2025-11-29).** He writes that the most supervision bits come from the statistical simulation of human text, which gives the primordial behaviours on top of which everything else is bolted, then increasing RL finetuning and selection by A/B tests for daily active users; see [[llms-are-shaped-by-commercial-not-biological-evolution]] ([[blog-2025-11-29-space-of-minds]] §intro).

**Midtraining deleted from base training (2026-01-31).** In the GPT-2 record's pipeline a BOS-aligned dataloader starts every row with `<|bos|>`, which, he says, "Made midtraining unnecessary (deleted it)." ([[github-2026-01-31-nanochat-beating-gpt-2]] §What Worked, §Training Script). **Note:** the launch post of 2025-10-13 describes midtraining as its own stage ([[github-2025-10-13-nanochat-discussion-introducing]] §Midtraining); this post removes it from the base-training pipeline as of its date and does not discuss SFT ([[github-2026-01-31-nanochat-beating-gpt-2]] §What Worked).

**From document completer to ChatGPT (microgpt, 2026-02-12).** The pretrained base model is a document completer, not a chatbot; SFT swaps the documents for curated conversations and keeps training, "Algorithmically, nothing changes.", and RL scores the model's responses by humans, a judge model or an algorithm and learns from the feedback ([[blog-2026-02-12-microgpt]] §Real stuff). Fundamentally, he says, the model is still training on documents, now made of tokens from the model itself ([[blog-2026-02-12-microgpt]] §Real stuff). Production data is trillions of tokens of web pages, books and code, deduplicated, quality-filtered and mixed ([[blog-2026-02-12-microgpt]] §Real stuff). ChatGPT is the same predict-sample-repeat loop scaled up, plus post-training; system prompt, message and reply are all tokens of one document it completes ([[blog-2026-02-12-microgpt]] §FAQ).

**ClimbMix (X post, 2026-03-05).** The biggest gain in nanochat's 2-hour GPT-2 run, he says, was switching the pretraining data from FineWeb-edu to NVIDIA ClimbMix; he had tried Olmo, FineWeb and DCLM, which all led to regressions, while "ClimbMix worked really well out of the box" ([[x-2026-03-05-nanochat-gpt-2-in-two-hours]]). It worked well enough that he is slightly suspicious of goodharting, though reading the paper it seems about okay to him; see [[llm-evaluation]] ([[x-2026-03-05-nanochat-gpt-2-in-two-hours]]).
