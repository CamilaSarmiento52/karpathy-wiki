# Pretraining and finetuning
Training ChatGPT, as he describes it in January 2023, has roughly two stages: pretraining, which trains a decoder-only Transformer on a large chunk of the internet to babble text, and finetuning, which aligns the result into an assistant ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:48:42, @1:49:14, @1:52:36).

## What he says
Pretraining is very similar to the lecture's tiny baby pretraining step ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:48:42, @1:49:14). His Shakespeare model has about 10 million parameters trained on about 1 million characters, roughly 300,000 tokens in OpenAI's vocabulary ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:49:14, @1:49:47). GPT-3's largest model has 175 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens, about a millionfold more data, and that is not even large by today's standards; training at that scale is a massive infrastructure challenge with thousands of GPUs ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:50:22, @1:50:56). He says GPT-3's architecture is nearly identical to what the lecture implemented, and its hyperparameters (layers, n_embd, heads, head size, batch size, learning rate) should be recognisable ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:50:22, @1:50:56). Models like GPT-3 are between 10,000 and 1 million times bigger depending on how you count ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:55:22).

After pretraining you get a document completer that babbles internet, may answer questions with more questions, and is unaligned ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:51:29, @1:52:02). The finetuning stage aligns it to be an assistant in roughly three steps: supervised finetuning on question-answer documents, perhaps thousands of examples; raters ranking responses to train a reward model; and PPO, a policy-gradient reinforcement-learning optimiser, finetuning the sampling policy to score high reward ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:52:36, @1:53:08, @1:53:43). Very large models are very sample efficient during finetuning ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:53:08). Much of this data is internal to OpenAI and much harder to replicate; nanoGPT focuses on pretraining ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:54:16). Anything that is not a plain document completer, such as task performance, alignment or sentiment, requires further finetuning stages, simple supervised finetuning or, as in ChatGPT, a reward model with rounds of PPO ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:55:22, @1:55:55).

**Four stages (State of GPT, May 2023).** At Microsoft Build he calls the recipe emerging, very new and still rapidly evolving, with four stages that follow each other serially: pretraining, supervised finetuning, reward modeling and reinforcement learning, each with its own dataset, objective and resulting model ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @00:35, @01:07). Pretraining is about 99 percent of the training compute, with internet-scale data, thousands of GPUs and possibly months; the other three stages use a few GPUs for hours or days ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @01:07, @01:41). His example data mixture is LLaMA's: CommonCrawl, C4 and higher-quality sets such as GitHub, Wikipedia and books, sampled in given proportions ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @01:41, @02:16). Documents are packed into B×T rows separated by end-of-text tokens, and at every position the Transformer predicts the next token, the actual next token supervising the weights ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @05:06, @05:41, @06:45). The plot to watch is the loss over time ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @07:48).

He says LLaMA 65B is significantly more powerful than GPT-3 175B because it trained on 1.4 trillion tokens instead of 300 billion, so a model's power should not be judged by its parameter count ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @03:56). His figures for Meta's run are about 2,000 GPUs for about 21 days, roughly several million dollars ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @04:31). The field noticed that pretraining learns powerful general representations that can be finetuned for downstream tasks with few examples, because predicting the next token forces the model to multitask; around GPT-2 people found that prompting with fake documents could work even better, which he says began an era of prompting over finetuning ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @07:48, @08:23, @08:56, @09:31).

Supervised finetuning collects small, high-quality datasets of prompts and ideal responses written by contractors, typically tens of thousands, and is still language modeling with only the training set changed ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @11:43). The contractors follow long labelling documentation asking them to be helpful, truthful and harmless ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @12:15, @12:48). Reward modeling has the SFT model write several completions per prompt, people rank them, and a Transformer is trained to predict a reward at a special readout token that agrees with the rankings ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @13:23, @13:57, @14:31). The reward model is not deployable as an assistant but can score any completion ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @15:04). In the RL stage the model, initialised from SFT, samples completions, the frozen reward model scores them, and the language-modeling loss on the sampled tokens is weighted by the reward ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @15:35, @16:08). In his account RLHF is reward modeling plus reinforcement learning, and ChatGPT is an RLHF model ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @12:48, @16:43).

**Base models and assistant models.** He distinguishes three kinds of model, base, SFT and RLHF; ChatGPT is RLHF and Vicuna-13B is SFT ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @16:43). The GPT-4 base model was never released; the GPT-4 served over the API is an assistant model ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @09:31, @10:05). A few-shot prompt laid out as a human–assistant exchange can make a base model act as an assistant, but not very reliably ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @10:36, @11:09). On the Berkeley Elo ranking he puts GPT-4 best by far, then Claude and GPT-3.5, which he says are RLHF models, and the rest, to his knowledge, SFT models ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @19:29, @20:02).

**RLHF models lose entropy.** He says RLHF models are not strictly an improvement on base models in some cases: they lose some entropy and give peakier samples with lower variation ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @18:21). So when you have n things and want more like them, he still prefers a base model; his example completes seven Pokémon names into many more, which he checked were fictitious ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @18:55, @19:29).

**Finetuning in practice.** Finetuning is becoming more accessible through parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA, which train small pieces and keep most of the model clamped, allowing low-precision inference for the frozen parts ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @35:00, @35:33, @36:07). It still needs more technical expertise, contractors or synthetic-data pipelines, and slows iteration; SFT is achievable, but he calls RLHF research territory, unstable and not beginner-friendly, and would not advise rolling your own ([[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @36:07, @36:40).

**Lossy compression of the internet (Intro to LLMs, November 2023).** In his general-audience talk he takes Llama 2 70B as the example because Meta released the weights, architecture and paper, unlike ChatGPT, which you only use through a web interface ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @00:33, @01:06). The architecture and forward pass are algorithmically understood and open; the magic, and the cost, is in obtaining the parameters ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @03:52). His figures for Llama 2 70B are roughly 10 TB of crawled internet text, about 6,000 GPUs for about 12 days and about $2 million ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @04:25, @04:57). He calls these rookie numbers next to frontier models, which are off by a factor of 10 or more and cost tens or hundreds of millions of dollars to train, whereas running a trained network is fairly cheap ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @06:02, @06:37). The network predicts the next word, as in "cat sat on a" followed by "mat" at 97 percent, and because prediction and compression are closely related he calls training a compression of the internet ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @06:37, @07:10, @07:43). Predicting the next word of a Wikipedia page about Ruth Handler requires knowing who she was and what she did, so the objective forces the network to learn a lot about the world ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @07:43, @08:16, @08:50). Sampled word by word, the network "dreams" internet documents: Java code, an Amazon product page with a made-up ISBN, a Wikipedia-like article about a fish that is roughly correct but not verbatim, so you never know which parts are memorized and which hallucinated ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @08:50, @09:25, @09:58, @10:30, @11:02).

**Finetuning into an assistant.** Pretraining yields internet document generators; finetuning keeps the same next-word objective but swaps the dataset for conversations written by people hired with labeling instructions, giving an assistant model ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @14:21, @14:55). Pretraining data is large but low quality; finetuning prefers quality over quantity, for example 100,000 high-quality conversations ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @16:04). He calls it remarkable, empirical and not fully understood that the finetuned model changes its format to a helpful assistant while still using the knowledge from pretraining; roughly, pretraining is about knowledge and finetuning about alignment ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @17:11). Pretraining costs millions of dollars and happens inside companies maybe once a year, while finetuning is much cheaper, perhaps a day, so companies iterate on it weekly or daily ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @17:45, @18:18, @18:50, @19:58). The finetuning loop is to evaluate, deploy, monitor misbehaviours, have a person write the correct response, add it to the training data and repeat ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @19:24). Meta's Llama 2 release includes both base and assistant models; the base model is not directly usable but lets you do your own finetuning ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @19:58, @20:30).

An optional stage three uses comparison labels, because comparing candidate answers is easier than writing one, for example picking the best haiku; at OpenAI this is called reinforcement learning from human feedback ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @21:03, @21:36, @22:09). The labeling instructions, from the InstructGPT paper, ask for helpful, truthful and harmless responses and can run to tens or hundreds of pages ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @22:09). Labeling is increasingly a human–machine collaboration, with models sampling answers, checking work or creating comparisons and people in oversight, a slider he says is moving to the right as models improve ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @22:42, @23:15). Chatbot Arena, from a Berkeley team, ranks models by Elo from blind pairwise votes ([[youtube-2023-11-23-intro-to-llms]] @23:15, @23:47).

**Special tokens in finetuning (tokenizer lecture, February 2024).** `<|endoftext|>` delimits documents in the pretraining stream ([[youtube-2024-02-20-gpt-tokenizer]] @1:19:34, @1:20:07). Finetuning a base model into a chat model introduces many new special tokens to hold the structure of a conversation between user and assistant, which requires small model surgery on the embedding and output layers, a very common operation ([[youtube-2024-02-20-gpt-tokenizer]] @1:21:49, @1:24:37, @1:25:09). His trailing-space demonstration uses `gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct`, which he describes as a completion model closer to a base model ([[youtube-2024-02-20-gpt-tokenizer]] @1:58:52).

**Finetuning regresses, components compose (Sequoia, 2024-03-26).** The more you finetune a model, the more it regresses on everything else; adding a capability without that wants training on a mixture of the old and new data, which needs the dataset and training loop, not just the weights ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @08:32). Asked about model composability, he says he sees papers on model merges but does not know that anything has really stuck ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @23:21). Neural nets are less composable than traditional code, but they do compose through pretraining: it is very common to pretrain components separately, plug them in and finetune through the whole, for example adding images to a ChatGPT-like system ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @23:53, @24:26). The AlphaGo and RLHF points from the same interview are on [[llm-self-improvement]] ([[talk-2024-03-26-sequoia-making-ai-accessible]] @24:59).

**Note:** the January lecture's three finetuning steps (SFT, reward model, PPO) are the same recipe this talk lays out as four stages with the last two named RLHF; more detail, not a change of view ([[youtube-2023-01-17-gpt-from-scratch]] @1:52:36; [[talk-2023-05-25-state-of-gpt]] @01:07).
