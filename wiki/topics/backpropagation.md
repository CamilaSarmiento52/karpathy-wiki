# Backpropagation
Backpropagation is an algorithm that efficiently evaluates the gradient of a loss function with respect to the weights of a neural network, so the weights can be tuned iteratively to minimise the loss ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @00:32, @01:05). He describes it as a recursive application of the chain rule backwards through an expression graph, starting at the output node and working back to every internal node and input ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @03:14, @50:37).

## What he says
He does not take the symbolic approach, because nobody writes out the derivative of a neural net, which would have tens of thousands of terms ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @09:45, @10:18). He defines the derivative as the limit of (f(x+h) - f(x))/h as h goes to zero: how the function responds, and with what sensitivity, when the input is nudged slightly ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @10:52). He estimates it numerically with a small h such as 0.001, as rise over run, noting that too small an h gives wrong answers because of finite floating-point precision ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @11:26, @12:35).

Because neural nets are massive expressions, a data structure is needed to maintain them; that is the purpose of micrograd's `Value` object ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @19:11). Each `Value` keeps `_prev`, the children that produced it, and `_op`, the operation, so every node knows how it came to be ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @22:29, @23:02, @24:08). The forward pass reads `.data` at the output; calling `backward` on the output fills `.grad` on every node ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @02:42, @03:14). He visualises graphs with graphviz through a `draw_dot` helper, adding fake op nodes so the picture reads nicely ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @25:13, @25:46, @26:18).

He states the chain rule as: if z depends on y and y depends on x, then dz/dx is dz/dy times dy/dx, and he prefers this formulation to the one he was taught, which he found confusing ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @41:47, @42:20). He borrows Wikipedia's intuition: if a car is twice as fast as a bicycle and the bicycle four times as fast as a walker, the car is eight times as fast as the walker ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @42:53, @43:27). Each node knows only its local derivative; the chain rule multiplies that local derivative onto the gradient of the output with respect to the node's output ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @40:39, @44:33, @50:37). A plus node routes the gradient unchanged to both children because its local derivatives are one, and he calls it "a plus is just a distributor of gradient"; a times node gives each input the other input's value times the incoming gradient ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @45:05, @1:04:28, @48:24, @1:06:42). He describes the gradient signal as flowing backwards through the graph ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @46:11).

Each `Value` carries a `grad` initialised to zero, meaning no effect on the output, and the base case is that the derivative of the output with respect to itself is one ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @30:39, @31:12, @32:18, @1:02:15). In the neural-net setting one wants the derivative of the loss with respect to the weights; derivatives with respect to the data are usually not used because the data is fixed ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @30:07, @30:39). Backpropagation is implemented by storing a `_backward` closure on each node that applies its piece of the chain rule; leaf nodes get an empty function ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @1:09:34, @1:10:08). Nodes must be processed so that everything after a node is done before it; this ordering is a topological sort of the DAG, and `backward` calls each node's `_backward` in reverse topological order ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @1:17:50, @1:18:24, @1:20:41). Gradients must be accumulated with `+=` rather than set, because a variable used more than once receives contributions from several paths; this follows from the multivariate chain rule ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @1:22:57, @1:24:33, @1:25:05).

The gradient tells you how to nudge inputs to make the output go up; nudging the leaves in the direction of the gradient raised the output from -8 to -7, which he presents as one step of optimisation ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @51:10, @52:18).

In PyTorch the computational graph is built during the forward pass, and `backward` fills in `W.grad` ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:39:36, @1:40:09). Every step of the bigram net's forward pass (multiply, add, exp, sum, divide) is differentiable, so the whole thing can be backpropagated through ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:24:35, @1:28:29).

### Backprop at the level of tensors
In the fourth makemore lecture he removes `loss.backward()` and writes the backward pass of the two-layer MLP with batchnorm by hand at the level of tensors, because micrograd worked on individual scalars, which he does not think is enough ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @00:31, @02:43). He says the exercise makes you better at debugging and makes everything fully explicit; writing the backward pass by hand is not recommended today and nobody does it except as an exercise, but about ten years earlier it was standard, including for him ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @03:16, @03:49). His 2010 restricted Boltzmann machine library was in MATLAB and used contrastive divergence; his 2014 fragment-embeddings code in numpy implemented the cost and backward pass by hand and used a gradient checker ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @04:26, @05:34, @06:08, @06:41). In the backward pass each tensor gets a `d`-prefixed counterpart holding the derivative of the loss with respect to it, always of the same shape ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @09:58, @13:13).

Working backwards: the gradient of the mean-of-negative-log-probs loss is -1/n at the plucked-out positions and zero everywhere else ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @15:35, @16:07). Backprop through log multiplies by 1/probs, which boosts the gradient of examples currently assigned low probability ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @19:27, @20:31). He uses `**-1` instead of division because he found something wrong with PyTorch's backward for division ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @21:38). A node used in two branches of the graph receives the sum of both contributions, implemented with `+=`, and an intermediate check is expected to fail until the second branch is done ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @27:18, @31:45, @1:03:34). He calls addition a router of gradient: whatever arrives is routed equally to every element that participated ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @30:39). Subtracting the per-row max for softmax stability does not change the loss, so its gradient should be zero; it comes out at about 1e-9 because of floating point ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @36:44, @37:17). `max` returns indices as well as values, and the backward pass scatters the gradient to those indices, which he implements with `F.one_hot` ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @38:55, @40:03).

Written out on paper, a 2×2 matmul plus bias is just multiplies and adds; the result is dL/dA = dL/dD @ Bᵀ, dL/dB = Aᵀ @ dL/dD, and dL/dC is a sum over the examples, so the backward pass of a matrix multiply is a matrix multiply with the other operand transposed ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @44:29, @47:49, @48:22, @48:57, @49:29). He does not remember these formulas; the dimensions have to work out, and there is only a single way to make the shapes work out, which is the correct one ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @50:01, @50:37). The backward of tanh is 1 minus the output squared, in terms of the output not the input ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @54:03, @54:35). The backward of `view` is just re-viewing the gradient into the original shape ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:21:06). The backward of the embedding lookup routes each gradient row back into the row of C it came from, adding when a row was used several times; he uses a Python for-loop and invites a vectorised version ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:23:52, @1:24:24).

### Broadcasting's backward pass
A broadcasted multiply like `counts * counts_sum_inv` is really two operations, replication of the column across 27 columns and then the element-wise multiply; replication means a variable is reused many times, so, as in micrograd, the gradients from all uses sum, and the backward pass is a `sum(1, keepdim=True)` so the gradient keeps the variable's shape ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @23:19, @23:54, @25:02, @25:34, @26:07). Conversely a forward `sum` over rows becomes a backward replication, which he implements as `torch.ones_like(counts) * dcounts_sum` ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @31:11, @31:45). He states the duality explicitly: a sum in the forward pass turns into replication or broadcasting in the backward pass along the same dimension, and a broadcast in the forward pass indicates variable reuse and turns into a sum over that dimension ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:09:12, @1:09:47).

### Cross-entropy in closed form
Exercise one backpropagated through far too many atomic pieces; if you differentiate the mathematical expression for the loss on paper, many terms cancel and the result is much shorter and faster, which is why PyTorch glues the whole thing into `F.cross_entropy` ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:26:39, @1:27:11). For one example the loss is -log of the softmax probability at the label; differentiating with respect to logit i splits into the case where i is the label and where it is not, giving p_i, or p_i - 1 at the label; in a batch this is the softmax of the logits, minus 1 at the label column, divided by n for the mean ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:28:50, @1:30:32, @1:31:36, @1:32:09). This matches PyTorch to about 5e-9, approximately rather than bitwise ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:32:09). Visualised, dlogits is the probability matrix with the correct positions reduced by 1, and each row sums to zero ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:32:44, @1:33:50). He reads each gradient cell as a force, pulling down the probabilities of incorrect characters and pulling up the correct one, with push and pull exactly balanced, and pictures the network as a pulley system in which tension at the logits translates into tugs on the weights and biases ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:33:50, @1:34:24, @1:34:57). The force is proportional to the probabilities from the forward pass: a perfectly correct prediction gives a row of zeros and no push or pull, while a confidently wrong one is pulled down heavily, which he calls the magic of the cross-entropy loss ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:35:31, @1:36:04).

### Batchnorm in closed form
Exercise one breaks batchnorm into mean, diff, diff squared, variance, inverse std and raw pieces and backpropagates through each line, using the power rule for the -0.5 exponent ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @59:38, @1:00:11, @1:04:07). bndiff feeds two branches, through bnraw and through the variance, so its gradient fails the check until the second branch is added; likewise hprebn receives gradient both directly and via the mean ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:03:34, @1:13:39, @1:16:31, @1:17:03). Exercise three treats batchnorm as one glued expression and derives dhprebn from dhpreact on two sheets of paper, ignoring gamma and beta because their gradients are simple ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:36:36, @1:37:42, @1:38:18). He warns that x, x-hat and y are vectors of many nodes while mu and sigma-squared are single scalars, and recommends backpropagating in the order x-hat, sigma-squared, mu, x, like a topological sort ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:38:50, @1:39:25). Because sigma-squared fans out into all the x-hats, its gradient is a sum over i of global times local; mu has 32 arrows to the x-hats plus one to sigma-squared, and the d(sigma-squared)/d(mu) term vanishes exactly when mu is the mean, which cancels the whole second term ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:41:03, @1:41:36, @1:42:09, @1:43:17). Each x_i has three arrows, to mu, sigma-squared and its own x-hat, and the final chain-rule sum requires renaming the summation index from i to j to avoid clashing with the x_i being differentiated ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:44:23, @1:46:37). The terms refactor so that (sigma-squared + eps)^(-3/2) splits into three factors and some terms collapse into x-hats, leaving an expression that uses only dL/dy and the x-hats from the forward pass ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:47:11, @1:47:45, @1:48:17). The one-line implementation matches PyTorch with a tiny max difference, but writing it was not trivial because the per-neuron formula must run in parallel over all 64 columns with every sum broadcasting correctly; he calls it the notable exception to the rest of the backward pass being about three lines per layer ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:48:17, @1:48:50, @1:49:24, @1:53:52).

Training with the manual gradients gives the same loss and samples as before, with full visibility into what happens under the hood; he says that in practice you probably do not want to derive these yourself and should use PyTorch's autograd, but should have the intuition ([[youtube-2022-10-11-makemore-4-backprop-ninja]] @1:50:31, @1:53:18, @1:54:25).
