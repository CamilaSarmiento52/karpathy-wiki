# Gradient descent
The gradient is a vector pointing in the direction of increasing loss, so parameters are nudged by a small step size times the negative gradient ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:02:10, @2:03:19).

## What he says
He first writes the update without the minus sign, reasons through a specific neuron, and adds the negative ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:02:44, @2:03:19). Training is forward pass, backward pass, update, repeated; his loss fell from 4.84 to 4.36 to 3.9 and so on ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:04:57, @2:05:30, @2:06:02). Too large a step overshoots because only the local dependence of the loss is known; his loss briefly exploded before landing at 7e-9 ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:06:35, @2:07:11, @2:07:43).

He calls the learning rate and its tuning "a subtle art": too low converges far too slowly, too high destabilises training and can explode the loss, depending on the loss function ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:06:35, @2:07:43). In the lecture 0.01 was too small and 0.1 dangerously high, so he picked in between for 20 steps ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:07:43, @2:09:21). A proper training loop zeros the gradients before `backward`, then updates ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:11:35, @2:12:09). He mentions, without explaining, batching on large datasets, L2 regularisation and learning-rate decay in micrograd's demo notebook ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @2:19:18, @2:19:51, @2:20:23).

In the bigram net, matrix multiplication evaluates all neurons on all examples in parallel; element (3, 13) is the dot product of input 3 with column 13 of W ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:15:34, @1:17:16, @1:17:50). Reseeding W and checking the loss is guess and check, which he calls amateur hour and "not how you optimize a neural net"; instead, commit to a random start and minimise the differentiable loss with gradients ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:31:15, @1:31:47, @1:32:20, @1:32:54). The loop mirrors micrograd: forward pass, loss, zero the gradients, `loss.backward()`, nudge parameters against the gradient ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:33:26, @1:34:33, @1:35:05). Setting `.grad` to None is a more efficient way to zero it, and W needs `requires_grad=True` ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:38:29, @1:39:03). A positive gradient means increasing that weight increases the loss ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:40:09, @1:40:43). The update is `W.data += -0.1 * W.grad`, and the loss falls 3.76, 3.74, 3.72 ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:41:15, @1:42:21). Scaling from 5 to 228,000 bigrams needs no code change, and a much larger learning rate (even 50) works on this simple problem, reaching about 2.47 ([[youtube-2022-09-07-makemore-1-bigram]] @1:43:27, @1:44:00, @1:44:36).

Minibatches arrive in the second makemore lecture: forwarding and backwarding all 228,000 examples each step is too slow, so in practice people update on a randomly selected minibatch, and he uses `torch.randint` to pick 32 indices into X and Y ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @41:35, @42:07, @42:42, @43:16). With minibatches the gradient quality is lower and the direction less reliable, but good enough on 32 examples to be useful; he says it is much better to have an approximate gradient and take more steps than an exact gradient with fewer ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @43:50, @44:24). The minibatch loss is noisy, so he evaluates on the full training set to judge progress ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @44:24, @44:56). The loss curve is thick because of minibatch noise; a larger batch size would give a more correct gradient and less thrashing, and plotting log10 of the loss squashes the hockey-stick shape ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @1:01:57, @1:02:30, @1:08:14).

On the learning rate, his initial 0.1 was a guess, and he cannot tell from the loss alone whether steps are too slow or too fast ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @44:56, @45:28). A rate of 1 minimises the loss but is unstable and 10 does not optimise at all, so the right value is between 0.001 and 1; stepping linearly between rates does not make sense, so he steps linearly in the exponent ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @46:34, @47:07, @47:42). The loss-versus-exponent plot shows nothing happening at low rates, a valley, then instability; around exponent -1, that is 0.1, is a good setting ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @49:52, @50:25, @50:59). Learning-rate decay means lowering the rate by 10x in the late stages of training once the loss plateaus ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @51:32, @52:04). His rough recipe is to find a decent rate, train a while, then decay by a factor of 10 and take a few more steps, which he calls janky and not production practice ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @52:37). He admits his decay is done at random and the optimisation is suspect; in production you would turn these into hyperparameters and run many experiments ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @1:09:20, @1:09:55). His final run uses 0.1 for 100,000 steps and then 0.01 for 100,000 ([[youtube-2022-09-12-makemore-2-mlp]] @1:11:38).

The third makemore lecture adds a way to judge the learning rate from the updates themselves. He tracks, per weight tensor, the update-to-data ratio, the standard deviation of learning rate times gradient divided by the standard deviation of the parameter, and plots its log10 over time ([[youtube-2022-10-04-makemore-3-activations-batchnorm]] @1:40:26, @1:41:00). He calls -3 on that scale, updates about a thousandth of the tensor's magnitude, a rough guide: well below it usually means parameters train too slowly, and with learning rate 0.001 the updates were 10,000× too small ([[youtube-2022-10-04-makemore-3-activations-batchnorm]] @1:42:06, @1:43:12, @1:43:45). Dead neurons can arise during training when a high learning rate knocks a neuron off the data manifold ([[youtube-2022-10-04-makemore-3-activations-batchnorm]] @21:58, @22:33). Changing the activation scale changes the update scale: with batch normalization and no fan-in scaling the ratio fell well below -3 and he raised the learning rate 10× to 1.0, and he admits he did not fully explain why a different gain needs a different learning rate ([[youtube-2022-10-04-makemore-3-activations-batchnorm]] @1:49:50, @1:50:24, @1:51:00, @1:54:48). He also mentions the earlier learning-rate finding mechanism and says the field has not settled the best update rule ([[youtube-2022-10-04-makemore-3-activations-batchnorm]] @1:53:45, @1:55:22).

In the WaveNet lecture he says the same of hyperparameter tuning as of weights: with no experimental harness they are just guessing and checking, and he says this is not representative of typical deep-learning workflows, which run scripted experiments, plot training and validation losses and search hyperparameters ([[youtube-2022-11-21-makemore-5-wavenet]] @46:42, @54:32).

**What a real reproduction needs (llm.c, May 2024).** Planning a proper reproduction of the GPT-2 miniseries in llm.c, he lists gradient accumulation, gradient clipping, and learning-rate warmup and schedule among the pieces still to build, alongside random initialisation in C, an evaluation and a modern dataset ([[github-2024-05-03-llmc-state-of-the-union]] §intro (Functional)). By the cross-check thread, llm.c takes a desired total batch size and computes the gradient-accumulation steps itself: micro-batch 16 × 1024 tokens against a total of 131072 gives 8 ([[github-2024-05-24-llmc-pytorch-cross-checks]] §1 GPU training in FP32). On 4 GPUs the total batch stays identical, so llm.c lowers the accumulation steps and everything else stays the same ([[github-2024-05-24-llmc-pytorch-cross-checks]] §multiGPU).

**GPT-3's recipe for GPT-2 (llm.c, 2024-05-28).** His settings for the 124M reproduction, as dated practice: many hyperparameters follow the GPT-3 paper rather than GPT-2's because it was much more detailed ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Args guide)). The total batch is about 0.5M tokens per update, from the GPT-3 paper's table for this model size since GPT-2's batch size is unknown; the code computes the gradient-accumulation inner loop to reach it, none on 8 GPUs at micro-batch 64 and 8 steps on one GPU ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Args guide)). Weight decay is 0.1 and applies only to 2-D weights, exactly as in GPT-2; the maximum learning rate is 6e-4; it ramps up from 0 to the maximum over the first 700 iterations, 350M tokens; and in this run it decays to 0 over training ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Args guide)). Gradient clipping at 1.0 is, he says, standard, used to mitigate gradient explosions ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Training)).

**His brief training guide (llama2.c).** He says he mostly only tunes a handful of settings and leaves the rest unchanged ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §training). Scale dim, n_layers and n_heads together, inter- or extrapolating the table at the end of the Chinchilla paper; set the max context to the number of tokens that matter for predicting the next one (Llama 2 uses 2048); aim for a total batch of about 100K tokens per update for medium applications, lower for tiny ones and usually about 0.5M or more for large GPT or Llama training, reached by maxing out the batch size and then raising gradient accumulation ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §training). Tune the learning rate as high as training allows: very small networks can take 1e-3 or more, large ones need lower, and 3e-4 is safe for most medium applications but can be too low for small networks ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §training). His 110M run, which he does not think is anywhere near optimal: dim 768, 12 layers, 12 heads, sequence length 1024, batch 16 with 8 accumulation steps for 131,072 tokens per update, learning rate 4e-4 (probably a little too low), 200K iterations (probably a bit too high), dropout 0.1, on 4 GPUs with DDP for about a day ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §training). **Note:** the about 0.5M tokens he gives for large training is the batch size llm.c's GPT-2 (124M) run takes from the GPT-3 paper ([[github-2024-05-29-llama2c-readme]] §training; [[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Args guide)).

**GPT-3's recipe, in PyTorch (GPT-2 lecture, 2024-06-09).** He switches from SGD to AdamW, which keeps first- and second-moment buffers (something like momentum and something like RMSProp), normalises each gradient element and optimises language models faster; he treats it as a black box here ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @56:46, @57:19, @57:51). He likes the AdamW variant specifically because, in his opinion, it is a bug fix of Adam; he does not explain the bug ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @57:19). `backward()` always adds to `.grad`, so you must start from zero gradients ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @58:28). He calls 3e-4 a pretty good default learning rate for early debugging ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @1:01:14).

The GPT-2 paper is vague on optimisation and OpenAI released only inference code, so he follows the GPT-3 paper: GPT-2 has weights but no details, GPT-3 details but no weights ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:15:20, @2:15:55). From the GPT-3 paper he takes Adam β1 0.9, β2 0.95 and ε 1e-8, and clips the global gradient norm at 1.0 ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:16:28, @2:17:02). Clipping takes the square root of the sum of all squared gradients and caps it, guarding against a bad batch shocking the model; he calls it a bit of a hacky solution, a patch on top of deeper issues, that people still use fairly often ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:17:37, @2:18:12, @2:18:49). The paper's schedule (its numbers) warms up linearly over 375M tokens and decays by cosine to 10% over 260B tokens, then continues at 10%; its table gives a maximum learning rate of 6e-4 for the 12-layer, 768-wide model, which he adopts ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:21:02, @2:21:35, @2:23:19). His schedule starts from a small nonzero rate, warms up, and decays to 10% of the maximum at the last step rather than at GPT-3's 260B of a 300B horizon, which he does not think matters much ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:24:26, @2:24:59, @2:25:33). Which schedule to use is up to you, he says: cosine was popularised by GPT-2 and GPT-3, and schedules are an active research area ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:26:05). He skips the paper's gradual batch-size ramp; his reasons are on [[compute-efficiency]] ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:26:05, @2:26:38). GPT-3 samples without replacement until an epoch boundary, which his loader already does ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:28:21).

In the GPT-3 table bigger networks use lower learning rates and bigger batches, and the 124M model about 0.5M tokens per batch; he wants that batch because batch size is coupled to the other optimisation hyperparameters ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:35:04, @2:35:40, @2:36:15). Gradient accumulation simulates it serially: he sets 2^19 = 524,288 tokens and runs 32 forward-backward passes of 16 × 1,024 tokens per step, about 3 seconds a step on one GPU ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:36:48, @2:37:23, @2:37:58, @2:38:32). The naive version is wrong, because summing gradients over micro-steps drops the mean's normaliser; he shows this on a toy MSE example and divides the loss by the number of accumulation steps ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:39:41, @2:41:57, @2:42:30, @2:43:04, @2:44:45). With accumulation the micro-batch size becomes purely a performance setting, and any value should give the same optimisation up to floating point ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:46:21). He says the relationships between weight decay, learning rate, batch size and Adam's parameters are complicated and that he is mostly copying OpenAI's settings ([[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:33:54, @2:34:29). **Note:** this run's decay to 10% differs from the llm.c 124M run's decay to 0 ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Args guide); [[youtube-2024-06-09-reproduce-gpt-2]] @2:24:59).

**How far to decay, dated.** His llm.c practice moves across three posts. On 2024-05-28 the 124M run decays the learning rate to 0 ([[github-2024-05-28-llmc-gpt-2-124m]] §intro (Args guide)). On 2024-06-12, after the 774M run, he writes that they should not decay to 0%, because the tail of the optimisation went way too slow, and that he will probably follow the GPT-3 paper and decay to 10% (`-q 0.1`) ([[github-2024-06-12-llmc-gpt-2-774m]] §intro (Reflections)). On 2024-07-11 the 1.6B run decays to 10% of the maximum with `-q 0.1`, following the GPT-3 paper ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Args guide)).

**The 1.6B recipe (llm.c, 2024-07-11).** The total batch is 2^20 ≈ 1M tokens, from the GPT-3 paper's table; with 8 GPUs × 16 × 1024 = 131,072 tokens per micro-step the code does 8 forward-backward passes per update ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Args guide)). The schedule is cosine (the default), maximum learning rate 6e-4, tripled from the GPT-3 paper's 2e-4, with a 700-step warmup; weight decay is 0.1 on 2-D weights only; and 32,000 steps were chosen as a nice number that fits 24 hours ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (Args guide)). He skips updates on instantaneous instability with `-sl 5.0 -sg 5.0` ([[github-2024-07-11-llmc-gpt-2-1-6b]] §intro (400B token run)).
