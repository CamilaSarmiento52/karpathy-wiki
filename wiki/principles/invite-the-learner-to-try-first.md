# Invite the learner to try a step before you show the answer

> "see if you can figure it out yourself" — [[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @1:33:56
>
> "This video is not super intended to be simply watched." — [[github-2024-02-20-nn-zero-to-hero-readme]] §Lecture 5

## In practice

In his lectures the invitation is brief and optional. In micrograd he poses the backward pass for the power function as an exercise, suggests pausing the video to figure it out, and then goes straight on to derive it ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @1:33:56). In the tokenizer lecture he invites viewers to implement the encoder themselves if they would like a fun exercise, and otherwise starts on his own solution in the same breath ([[youtube-2024-02-20-gpt-tokenizer]] @48:38).

Only for a longer exercise does he ask for real work first. The Zero to Hero README asks learners to work through the backprop exercise of lecture 5 themselves, with the video alongside, and to unpause and see him give the answer whenever they are stuck ([[github-2024-02-20-nn-zero-to-hero-readme]] §Lecture 5).

In the Dwarkesh interview, describing what good educators do, the speaker says presenting the solution before the student has a go is wasteful, because trying it yourself gives a better understanding ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @2:19:57). The raw has no speaker labels: the speaker is inferred from the turn structure, and the "I" is the educator he describes, not a report of his own lectures ([[talk-2025-10-17-dwarkesh-ghosts-not-animals]] @2:19:57, @2:20:34).

When teaching, pose the next step as a question and offer the learner a chance to try. Give the answer as soon as they ask for it or are stuck, and never make them guess before they may proceed. For a longer exercise, like the README's backprop exercise, set the work first and keep the solution available ([[youtube-2022-08-16-micrograd]] @1:33:56, [[github-2024-02-20-nn-zero-to-hero-readme]] §Lecture 5).
