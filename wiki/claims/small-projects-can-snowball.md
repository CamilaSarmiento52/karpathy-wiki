# Small, fragile projects can snowball into very big ones
> "it's very easy to dismiss these small snowballs because they're so fragile" — [[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @14:50

## Context
Talking to hackathon teams, he says the first thing he finds really incredible is how very small projects can snowball into really big ones ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @11:09). Several of his early projects, a Rubik's Cube color extractor and a janky neuroevolution simulator, did not snowball; he does not call that wasted work and says it still helped him along the way ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @11:47, @12:23).

**His teaching, as he tells it.** His game-programming tutorials on YouTube, about 13 years before the talk, led to Rubik's Cube videos that became popular and sparked his interest in teaching; as a Stanford PhD student he developed and taught CS231n, which he calls the first big deep-learning class at Stanford; then came the Zero to Hero series and now LLM101n, which he presents as the same snowball continuing ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @11:47, @12:23, @13:01, @13:39).

**OpenAI's Reddit chatbot.** Early at OpenAI, when eight people worked out of Greg's apartment as a counterbalance to Google, someone worked on a Reddit chatbot that looked easy to dismiss next to the goal of competing with Google ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @13:39, @14:14, @14:50). He says it was really a language model that happened to be trained on Reddit; after the Transformer and a wider web domain it led to GPT-1 through GPT-4 and GPT-4o ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @14:50, @15:27). He tells the teams that some of their projects probably will not go anywhere and some probably will, and that they should keep the momentum ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @16:04).
