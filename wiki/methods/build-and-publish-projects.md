# Build and publish projects
His advice to builders at the Berkeley hackathon: your brain is a reward machine and needs rewards, and his way of giving it rewards is doing projects and publishing them, good and bad ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @19:48, @20:24).

## Steps
1. **End to end.** Projects make you work on something end to end and in depth, unlike classes, which teach breadth-wise, just in case you need it ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @20:24).
2. **Learn on demand.** On a project you know what you need and learn it on demand; he calls this a very different mode that complements breadth-wise learning ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @20:24).
3. **Remix what you have.** His weekend movie site went up quickly because he already had code snippets lying around from earlier projects and remixed them ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @17:53).
4. **Publish it.** He keeps publishing projects, hackathon and random ones, and says not all of them are good ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @19:48, @20:24).
5. **Let the audience raise the bar.** Putting projects up is, in his experience, a really good Jedi mind trick: thinking of friends, teammates, family and future employers looking at it raises the bar and makes you work harder ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @20:24, @21:02).
6. **Take the reward.** When people like your project, fork it and build on it, that feels good to your brain ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @21:02).
7. **Count the hours.** The rewards keep you going toward 10,000 hours of experience, which is what lets a small project snowball into a big one ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @21:39, @16:04).

His current example of a project still at the small end is LLM101n, his next class: building a storyteller, something like a ChatGPT for generating stories, from basic prerequisites to a ChatGPT clone in that domain, which he says he had put on GitHub two or three days earlier and was still raw ([[talk-2024-07-03-berkeley-hackathon-keynote]] @13:01).

The [[llm101n]] README snapshot, dated 2024-08-01 by its commit, says the course does not yet exist, that it is being developed by Eureka Labs, and that he is archiving the repo until it is ready ([[github-2024-08-01-llm101n-readme]] §intro).

His llm.c talk covered how open-source development attracts "Avengers from the internet", his word for contributors ([[x-2024-10-02-gpu-mode-talk]]).

In the CUDA MODE keynote he calls people coming from the internet to help one of his favourite things that can happen with an open-source MIT-licensed repo; llm.c gathered about 60 contributors, and he says "we learned a lot and we made friends along the way" ([[talk-2024-10-03-cuda-mode-keynote]] @13:11, @13:44, @19:50).
